Matter & Chemical Change

Everything around you is made of tiny particles. In this unit you'll learn how to work safely with chemicals, what atoms and elements are, how to read the periodic table, and how to tell when a brand-new substance has been made.

2The particle model and states of matterSolids, liquids and gases›
I can use the particle model to explain solids, liquids and gases. B3
All matter is made of tiny particles that are always moving. Adding heat makes them move faster and spread out.
SolidPacked tight, vibrate in place. Keeps its shape.
LiquidClose but sliding past each other. Takes the container's shape.
GasFar apart, moving fast. Fills the whole space.

Example A snowbank in spring

  1. Snow (solid) warms up → particles move faster → melts into water (liquid).
  2. Puddles warm up more → evaporate into water vapour (gas).
  3. Same water particles, just more energy and more space between them.

3Elements, compounds and mixturesSorting all matter›
I can tell the difference between elements, compounds and mixtures. B1
An element has only one kind of atom. A compound has two or more kinds of atoms chemically joined. A mixture is substances mixed together but not joined.
TypeWhat it isExamples
ElementOne kind of atomIron (Fe), oxygen (O), gold (Au), carbon (C)
CompoundDifferent atoms chemically joinedWater (H₂O), table salt (NaCl), carbon dioxide (CO₂)
MixtureSubstances mixed, not joinedAir, salt water, concrete

Example Reading a formula

H₂O means 2 hydrogen atoms joined to 1 oxygen atom. Two kinds of atoms joined together, so water is a compound.

4Atoms and the periodic tableProtons, neutrons, electrons›
I can name the parts of an atom and read the periodic table. B3
An atom has a nucleus in the middle with protons (+) and neutrons (no charge). Tiny electrons (−) move around the outside.
Atom model● proton (+) ● neutron ● electron (−)
Periodic table square6 = atomic number (number of protons)

Reading the periodic table

  • Elements are in order by atomic number, the number of protons.
  • Each element has a symbol: C = carbon, O = oxygen, Fe = iron, Na = sodium, Au = gold.
  • Metals are on the left and middle (shiny, conduct electricity). Non-metals are on the right.
  • Elements in the same column (group) act in similar ways.

Example Oxygen

Oxygen's atomic number is 8, so an oxygen atom has 8 protons. In a neutral atom the electrons match the protons, so it also has 8 electrons.

5Physical and chemical changeIs it a new substance?›
I can tell a physical change from a chemical change using evidence. B2
A physical change changes how something looks, but it's still the same substance (melting, cutting, dissolving). A chemical change makes a new substance and is usually hard to undo.

Evidence of a chemical change Look for…

  • a new colour (rust on a bike)
  • bubbles or gas (baking soda + vinegar)
  • a new smell (rotting food)
  • heat or light given off (a campfire)
  • a solid forming in a liquid (soap scum)

Example Toast

Bread turns brown, smells different and you can't turn it back into bread. Toasting is a chemical change. Cutting the bread in half is a physical change: it's still bread.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Change Hunter

Find 3 physical changes and 3 chemical changes at home or around town this week. Photograph or draw each one and list your evidence.

6Reactants, products and word equationsWriting what happens in a reaction›
I can write simple word equations with reactants and products. B4
Reactants are what you start with. Products are the new substances made. The arrow means "makes."
reactants → products

Four reactions to know

iron + oxygen → iron oxide (rust)
carbon + oxygen → carbon dioxide  ·  C + O₂ → CO₂
hydrogen + oxygen → water
sodium + chlorine → sodium chloride (table salt)

Example A campfire

Wood burns using oxygen from the air. Reactants: wood + oxygen. Products: carbon dioxide, water vapour, ash, heat and light.

Environmental Chemistry

Some chemicals keep us alive and others can make us sick. In this unit you'll learn which substances living things need, how harmful chemicals get into air, water and food chains, how scientists measure pH and quality, and how to handle and dispose of chemicals safely at home and at work.

I can identify substances living things need and plan a balanced diet. C1
Living things need certain chemicals. Organic substances contain carbon and come from living things (proteins, carbohydrates, fats, vitamins). Inorganic substances don't (water, and minerals like calcium and iron).

The food guide plate shows half vegetables and fruit, a quarter whole grains and a quarter protein foods, with water as the drink of choice. Traditional First Nations and Métis diets of game, fish and wild plants are rich in protein and nutrients too.

2How chemicals spreadDilution and biomagnification›
I can explain how chemicals enter living things and spread through food chains. C1
Chemicals get into living things by ingestion (eating, drinking) or absorption (through skin, roots or gills). Dilution spreads a chemical out in water or air. But some toxins build up: in biomagnification, each step up a food chain has more of the toxin stored in its body.

Example Mercury in fish

Mercury in a lake is very diluted in the water. Small fish take in a little, big fish eat many small fish, and the mercury builds up. That's why health agencies advise limiting how much big predator fish (like pike or walleye from some lakes) people eat.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Fish Advisory

3Acids, bases and pHThe pH scale›
I can use pH to describe acids and bases and their effects. C2
The pH scale goes from 0 to 14. Below 7 is acidic, 7 is neutral and above 7 is basic. The farther from 7, the stronger. We measure pH with indicator paper, indicator solutions (like red cabbage juice) or a pH meter.

Example 1 Antacids

Heartburn is stomach acid in the wrong place. An antacid is a base. It neutralizes the acid, moving the pH closer to 7.

Example 2 Acid rain

Burning coal and gasoline releases gases that turn rain acidic. Acid rain can harm lakes, forests and even wear away stone buildings.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Cabbage Indicator

Scientists monitor (test again and again) air and water to catch problems early. Governments set laws and guidelines for how clean air and drinking water must be, and what businesses are allowed to release.

5Hazardous chemicalsTransport, store, dispose›
I can transport, store and dispose of hazardous chemicals safely. C3
Hazardous chemicals need care at every step. At work, WHMIS labels and Safety Data Sheets (SDS) explain the hazards. Trucks and trains carrying dangerous goods use placards with a class number so emergency crews know what's inside.

Safe steps

  • Transport: sealed, upright, labelled containers. Never in food or drink bottles.
  • Store: original container, cool place, flammables away from heat, acids away from bases.
  • Dispose: household hazardous waste round-ups or eco-stations. Never down drains, storm sewers or in the garbage.
  • E-waste and batteries: take to a recycling drop-off. Batteries can leak and start fires in garbage trucks.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Eco-Station Guide

Find where your community takes household hazardous waste. Make a one-page guide for your family listing what goes there and when it's open.

Unit 3

Electrical Principles

Phones, furnaces, power tools and the whole Alberta power grid run on electricity. In this unit you'll follow energy as it changes form, build circuits, learn about volts, amps and ohms, stay safe around electricity, and figure out what your devices really cost to run.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • describe forms of energy and how devices convert them D1
  • tell static from current electricity, and conductors from insulators D2
  • build series and parallel circuits and use volts, amps and ohms D2
  • calculate energy use and find ways to waste less D3
  • compare ways of making electricity and their effects D4
⚡ Career connection: electricians, power line technicians, wind and solar technicians, and power plant operators are in demand across Alberta.

2Lessons

1Energy changes formInputs and outputs›
I can describe forms of energy and how devices convert them. D1
Energy can't be made or destroyed. It only changes form: chemical (stored in fuel, food, batteries), electrical, mechanical (motion), thermal (heat), light and sound.

Example A flashlight

Battery (chemical) → wires (electrical) → bulb (light + some thermal). The heat is wasted energy, since it isn't the output you wanted.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Lemon Battery

With an adult, make a wet cell from a lemon (or potato), a copper coin and a galvanized nail. Connect a few in a row and try to light a small LED.

2Static, current and safetyConductors and insulators›
I can tell static from current electricity, and conductors from insulators. D2
Static electricity is charge that builds up and jumps all at once (a shock from a doorknob, lightning). Current electricity is a steady flow through a circuit. Conductors (copper, water, people) let current flow. Insulators (rubber, plastic, dry wood) block it.

Danger depends on voltage and current

A static shock can be thousands of volts but almost no current, so it just stings. A wall outlet is 120 volts with enough current to kill. Power lines are much higher. Stay at least 10 m from downed power lines and call 911.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Safety Sweep

Do an electrical safety check at home with an adult: overloaded power bars, frayed cords, outlets near water. Write down 3 things that are safe and 1 thing to fix.

3CircuitsSeries, parallel, volts, amps, ohms›
I can build series and parallel circuits and use volts, amps and ohms. D2
A series circuit has one path: if one bulb goes out, they all go out. A parallel circuit has several paths: the others stay on (that's how homes are wired). Voltage (V, volts) is the push, current (I, amps) is the flow and resistance (R, ohms Ω) slows it down. V = I × R

Example

A 9 V battery is connected to a 3 Ω resistor. Current: I = V ÷ R = 9 ÷ 3 = 3 A.

Quick check

Expedition: Circuit Builder

4Energy use at homeWatts, kilowatt-hours and saving›
I can calculate energy use and find ways to waste less. D3
Power is measured in watts (W). Your power bill charges for kilowatt-hours (kWh): kWh = watts × hours ÷ 1,000. Then cost = kWh × price per kWh. Efficiency means more useful output for the same input.

Example Old bulb vs. LED

A 60 W bulb on for 5 hours: 60 × 5 ÷ 1,000 = 0.3 kWh. At $0.15/kWh that's $0.045 a day.
A 9 W LED gives the same light: 9 × 5 ÷ 1,000 = 0.045 kWh = about $0.007 a day. That's over 80% less.

I can compare ways of making electricity and their effects. D4
Nonrenewable sources will run out (natural gas, coal, oil). Renewable sources are replaced naturally (wind, sun, moving water). Every source has trade-offs in cost, reliability and environmental impact.

Unit 4

Biological Diversity

From prairie grasses to grizzly bears, Alberta is full of different living things, and they all depend on each other. In this unit you'll explore diversity within and between species, how traits are passed on, how farmers and nature "select" traits, and how people can protect species at risk.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • describe diversity within and among species A1
  • explain habitat, niche, population and community, and how species depend on each other A1
  • compare asexual and sexual reproduction A2
  • tell inherited from non-inherited traits and compare natural and artificial selection A2
  • explain how people affect species survival and how we can help A3
🌾 Career connection: farmers, ranchers, wildlife officers, park interpreters, vet techs and greenhouse workers work with living diversity every day.

2Lessons

1What is biodiversity?Variety of life›
I can describe diversity within and among species. A1
Biological diversity (biodiversity) is the variety of living things. A species is a group that can breed and have offspring that can also breed. Diversity among species: wolves, moose, aspens. Diversity within a species: all the different breeds of dogs, or the differences between people.

Example Why diversity matters

If every tree in a forest were the same, one disease or insect (like the mountain pine beetle) could wipe them all out. A diverse forest recovers better.

🧭

3ReproductionAsexual vs. sexual›
I can compare asexual and sexual reproduction. A2
Asexual reproduction needs one parent. Offspring are exact copies. Sexual reproduction needs two parents. Offspring get a mix of traits from both, so each one is different.

4Traits and selectionInherited or not? Natural or artificial?›
I can tell inherited from non-inherited traits and compare natural and artificial selection. A2
Inherited traits come from parents (eye colour, blood type). Non-inherited traits come from your life (a scar, a haircut, skills you learn). In natural selection, the environment decides which traits help survival. In artificial selection, people choose which animals or plants breed.

Example 1 Natural selection

Birds with beaks that suit the local food survive and have more chicks. Over many generations, that beak shape becomes more common.

Example 2 Artificial selection

Dairy farmers breed the cows that give the most milk. Canola was bred in Canada from rapeseed to be healthier to eat. Wheat varieties are bred to handle Alberta's short growing season.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Trait Survey

Survey 10 people for 3 inherited traits (attached earlobes, can roll tongue, dimples). Make a bar graph. Bonus: help a Grade 8 classmate graph theirs.

5Protecting speciesHabitat loss and solutions›
I can explain how people affect species survival and how we can help. A3
The biggest threat to species is habitat loss from farming, cities, roads and industry. When a species can't adapt fast enough, its population drops. A species can become endangered or extinct (gone forever).

Example Alberta species

Grizzly bears need huge areas without many roads. Woodland caribou lose habitat to cut lines and development. Swift foxes disappeared from Canada and were brought back by captive breeding programs, with foxes raised in captivity and released on the prairie starting in the 1980s.

Ways to help

  • Protect habitat: parks, wildlife corridors and overpasses (like the ones over the highway in Banff).
  • Captive breeding and seed banks.
  • Laws that protect species at risk.
  • Personal choices: stay on trails, don't feed wildlife, plant native plants.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Species Spotlight

Pick an Alberta species at risk. Make a poster or short video: where it lives, why it's at risk and one thing people can do to help. Share it with a younger class.

Unit 5

Space Exploration

GPS on your phone, weather forecasts and even memory-foam pillows come from space technology. In this unit you'll explore how people have understood the sky, how the Sun, Earth and Moon move, how astronauts survive in space, and what Canada has added to space exploration.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

  • describe the challenges of living in space and the spinoffs we use every day E2
  • discuss the risks of space exploration and Canada's contributions E3
🚀 Career connection: satellite technicians, aerospace engineers, avionics mechanics and GIS (mapping) technicians all use space technology.

2Lessons

1Looking upStories, science and telescopes›
I can describe cultural and scientific ways of understanding the sky, and the tools we use to explore it. E1
People have always used the sky to tell time, find their way and pass on stories. Many First Nations used the stars and Moon to track seasons for hunting and gathering. Today, telescopes, satellites and space probes let us see much farther.

Example Tools for exploring space

  • Optical telescopes collect visible light with lenses or mirrors.
  • Radio telescopes pick up radio waves from space, day or night.
  • Space telescopes like the James Webb Space Telescope sit above Earth's air for a clearer view. Canada built one of its key instruments.
  • Probes and rovers travel to other planets and send back data.

Quick check

🧭

2Our solar systemPlanets, moons and more›
I can compare bodies in the solar system with Earth. E1
The solar system is the Sun and everything that orbits it: 8 planets, their moons, dwarf planets like Pluto, asteroids and comets. The four inner planets are rocky. The four outer planets are giant balls of gas and ice.

3Sun, Earth and MoonPhases, seasons and tides›
I can explain moon phases, seasons, tides and how space events affect Earth. E1
Moon phases: the Moon doesn't make light. We see the sunlit part change as it orbits Earth about every 29.5 days. Seasons: Earth is tilted, so for part of the year our half leans toward the Sun (summer) and part leans away (winter). Tides: the Moon's gravity pulls on the oceans.

Example Northern lights

A solar flare blasts charged particles toward Earth. They hit our upper atmosphere and glow as the aurora borealis, often seen in northern Alberta. Big solar storms can also disrupt GPS, radio and power grids.

4Living in spaceLife support and spinoffs›
I can describe the challenges of living in space and the spinoffs we use every day. E2
Space has no air, extreme temperatures, radiation and microgravity (floating), which weakens bones and muscles. On the International Space Station (ISS), life-support systems recycle air and even turn sweat and urine back into drinking water.

5Canada in spaceRisks, rewards and big decisions›
I can discuss the risks of space exploration and Canada's contributions. E3
Space exploration brings risks: dangerous launches and re-entry, huge costs, and space junk (old satellites and pieces orbiting at high speed). It also raises big questions: Who owns resources on the Moon? How do we keep space clean?

Canadian contributions

  • Canadarm (first flown in 1981) and Canadarm2 on the ISS: robotic arms that catch and move cargo.
  • Chris Hadfield became the first Canadian to command the ISS in 2013.
  • Jeremy Hansen was chosen for Artemis II, the first crewed mission around the Moon since 1972.

Matter & Chemical Change Cheat Sheet · Grade 9 Science

Safety: Household symbols (octagon = contents, triangle = container; Caution → Warning → Danger). WHMIS = red diamond pictograms + Safety Data Sheet. At work you have the right to know, to take part and to refuse unsafe work.

Particle model: all matter is made of moving particles. Solid = packed, vibrating · Liquid = close, sliding · Gas = far apart, fast. Heat makes particles move faster.

WordMeaningExample
ElementOne kind of atomIron (Fe), oxygen (O)
CompoundDifferent atoms chemically joinedWater (H₂O), salt (NaCl)
MixtureMixed, not joinedAir, salt water
Proton / neutron / electron+ / no charge / −Atomic number = protons
Physical changeSame substanceMelting, dissolving
Chemical changeNew substanceRusting, burning

Evidence of chemical change: new colour · bubbles/gas · new smell · heat or light · a solid forms

Word equations (reactants → products): iron + oxygen → rust · carbon + oxygen → carbon dioxide · hydrogen + oxygen → water · sodium + chlorine → sodium chloride

Environmental Chemistry Cheat Sheet · Grade 9 Science

Organic: contains carbon, from living things (protein, carbohydrates, fats). Inorganic: water and minerals (calcium, iron).

Food Guide plate: ½ vegetables and fruit · ¼ whole grains · ¼ protein foods · water to drink.

WordMeaning
IngestionTaking in by eating or drinking
AbsorptionTaking in through skin, roots or gills
DilutionSpreading out, becoming less concentrated
BiomagnificationToxins building up at each step of a food chain
NeutralizeMix an acid and base to move pH toward 7

pH: 0–6 acid · 7 neutral · 8–14 base. Farther from 7 = stronger.

Hazardous chemicals: read the label and SDS · original containers · acids away from bases · dispose at an eco-station.

Electrical Principles Cheat Sheet · Grade 9 Science

Forms of energy: chemical · electrical · mechanical · thermal · light · sound. Energy changes form; it isn't made or destroyed.

Static = charge that builds up and jumps. Current = steady flow. Conductors let current flow (metal, water). Insulators block it (rubber, plastic).

QuantitySymbolUnit
Voltage (push)Vvolts (V)
Current (flow)Iamps (A)
ResistanceRohms (Ω)

V = I × R · I = V ÷ R · R = V ÷ I

Series: one path, one out = all out. Parallel: many paths (how homes are wired).

Energy cost: kWh = watts × hours ÷ 1,000. Cost = kWh × price.

Renewable: wind, solar, hydro. Nonrenewable: natural gas, coal, oil.

Biological Diversity Cheat Sheet · Grade 9 Science

Biodiversity: the variety of living things. Species: can breed and have offspring that can also breed.

WordMeaning
HabitatWhere it lives
NicheIts role or job
PopulationAll of one species in an area
CommunityAll the populations in an area

Asexual: 1 parent, identical copies (fission, budding, runners). Sexual: 2 parents, a mix of traits.

Inherited traits come from parents. Non-inherited traits come from life experience.

Natural selection: the environment picks. Artificial selection: people pick.

Threats: habitat loss is the biggest. Help: protected areas, wildlife corridors, captive breeding, laws, careful choices.

Space Exploration Cheat Sheet · Grade 9 Science

Planets in order: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars (rocky) · Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune (gas and ice giants). "My Very Eager Mother Just Served Us Nachos."

Moon phases: new → crescent → first quarter → gibbous → full, about 29.5 days for the whole cycle.

Seasons come from Earth's tilt. Tides come from the Moon's gravity. Auroras come from solar particles hitting our atmosphere.

Space challenges: no air, extreme temperatures, radiation, microgravity. The ISS recycles air and water.

Spinoffs: GPS · weather forecasts · memory foam · water filters.

Canada: Canadarm and Canadarm2 · Chris Hadfield (ISS commander, 2013) · Jeremy Hansen (Artemis II) · RADARSAT.

Risks: launch and re-entry, cost, space junk.

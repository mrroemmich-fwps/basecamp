Unit 1

Number Sense

Big numbers show up everywhere: house prices, populations, paycheques, kilometres on a truck. In this unit you'll read, write, compare and round numbers up to a million, and learn to tell when an answer makes sense.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • explain the value of each digit in a number up to 1,000,000 Number 1, 3
  • read and write big numbers in numerals and in words Number 1
  • compare and order whole numbers Number 2
  • round and estimate to make quick decisions Number 1, 12
  • check whether a calculator answer makes sense Number 12

2Lessons

1Place value to a millionEvery digit has a job›
I can explain the value of each digit in a number up to 1,000,000. Number 1, 3
A digit's place tells you its value. Each place is worth 10 times the place to its right.
Hundred thousandsTen thousandsThousandsHundredsTensOnes
472315

Example 472,315

  • The 4 is worth 400,000
  • The 7 is worth 70,000
  • The 2 is worth 2,000
472,315 = 400,000 + 70,000 + 2,000 + 300 + 10 + 5

2Reading and writing big numbersNumerals and words›
I can read and write big numbers in numerals and in words. Number 1
Read big numbers in groups of three, separated by commas or spaces. Say the group, then its name: thousand, then the rest.

Example 1 Numerals to words

305,040 → three hundred five thousand forty

Example 2 Words to numerals

"Sixty-two thousand eight" → 62 thousand and 8 ones → 62,008. The zeros hold the empty places.

Example 3 On a cheque

Cheques and some forms ask for the amount in words so no one can change it: $1,250 is written one thousand two hundred fifty dollars.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Cheque Writer

With an adult, look at a sample cheque or a form that asks for an amount in words. Write 3 amounts over $1,000 in words with no spelling mistakes.

3Comparing and orderingWhich is bigger?›
I can compare and order whole numbers. Number 2
Compare numbers from left to right. First check how many digits; then compare the biggest place first. Use > (greater than) and < (less than).

Example Three trucks for sale

Kilometres driven: 84,500 · 48,900 · 84,050
  1. All have 5 digits. Compare ten thousands: 8, 4, 8. So 48,900 is smallest.
  2. 84,500 vs 84,050: compare hundreds: 5 > 0.
  3. Least to greatest: 48,900 < 84,050 < 84,500

Try it

🧭
Expedition: Price Ranking

Find 5 used vehicles or houses for sale online with an adult. Order them from least to greatest price. Which would you choose, and why?

4Rounding and estimatingClose enough to decide fast›
I can round and estimate to make quick decisions. Number 1, 12
To round, look at the digit just to the right of the place you're rounding to. 5 or more → round up. 4 or less → stay.

Example 1 Round 47,382 to the nearest thousand

  1. Thousands digit: 7. Digit to the right: 3.
  2. 3 is 4 or less, so stay: 47,000

Example 2 Estimating a total

A family buys a fridge for $1,189, a stove for $879 and a microwave for $219. Round to the nearest hundred: 1,200 + 900 + 200 = about $2,300.

Try it

🧭
Expedition: Grocery Estimator

Go shopping with an adult. Round each item to the nearest dollar and keep a running estimate in your head. How close were you to the real total?

5Does it make sense?Estimate before you calculate›
I can check whether a calculator answer makes sense. Number 12
Calculators don't make mistakes, but people press the wrong buttons. Estimate first, then compare.

Example A missed digit

Jordan adds 4,890 + 2,105 and the calculator shows 2,594.
  1. Estimate: 5,000 + 2,000 = about 7,000.
  2. 2,594 is way off. Jordan typed 489 instead of 4,890.
  3. Real answer: 6,995, close to the estimate.

Quick check

Unit 2

Integers & Temperature

In an Alberta winter, numbers go below zero all the time. In this unit you'll use positive and negative numbers, read thermometers, figure out how much the temperature changed, and switch between Celsius and Fahrenheit.

  • find the difference between two temperatures Number 13, 14
  • use a chart to compare Celsius and Fahrenheit Number 13

2Lessons

1Positive and negative numbersAbove and below zero›
I can describe where positive and negative numbers are used. Number 14
Positive numbers are above zero. Negative numbers are below zero and have a minus sign: −5. Positive and negative whole numbers are called integers.

Find 5 places integers show up in your life this week (weather app, a bank balance, sports stats, an elevator with basement floors). Write each one as an integer.

2The number lineOrdering integers›
I can order positive and negative numbers on a number line. Number 14
On a number line, numbers get bigger to the right and smaller to the left. A thermometer is just a number line standing up.

Example Which is colder?

−7°C or −3°C? −7 is farther left on the number line, so −7°C is colder. With negatives, the bigger the number after the minus sign, the colder it is.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Weather Week

Record the low temperature in your town for 7 days. Put the temperatures in order on a number line and circle the coldest day.

3Temperature changesRising and falling›
I can find a new temperature after it rises or falls. Number 13, 14
Rising means move up (right) on the number line. Falling means move down (left). Crossing zero is fine; just keep counting.

Example 1 Rising past zero

It's −6°C and rises 10°. Count up 6 to reach 0, then 4 more: 4°C.

Example 2 Falling below zero

It's 3°C and falls 8°. Count down 3 to reach 0, then 5 more: −5°C.

Try it

🧭
Expedition: Morning to Afternoon

For 5 days, record the temperature before school and after school. Calculate how much it rose or fell each day.

4Temperature differencesHow far apart?›
I can find the difference between two temperatures. Number 13, 14
To find the difference between a negative and a positive temperature, count to zero, then count past it, and add the two parts.

Example A chinook

A chinook wind blows in and the temperature goes from −18°C to 7°C.
  1. −18 up to 0 is 18 degrees.
  2. 0 up to 7 is 7 degrees.
  3. 18 + 7 = 25 degrees warmer

Try it

🧭
Expedition: Chinook Watch

Find a news story or weather record about a big Alberta chinook. How many degrees did the temperature change, and how fast?

Decimals & Money

2Lessons

1Tenths and hundredthsDimes and pennies›
I can explain tenths and hundredths using money. Number 3
After the decimal point: the first place is tenths (dimes, 10¢) and the second is hundredths (cents, 1¢).

4 dollars, 7 dimes and 5 cents. $4.75 = 4 + 0.7 + 0.05. Canada stopped making pennies, so cash totals round to the nearest 5¢, but cards and receipts still use every cent.

2Comparing decimalsWhich costs more?›
I can compare and order decimals. Number 6
Line up the decimal points, add zeros so they have the same number of places, then compare from left to right.

Example 0.5 or 0.45?

  1. Write 0.5 as 0.50.
  2. Compare 0.50 and 0.45: tenths 5 > 4.
  3. 0.5 is greater, even though 45 looks bigger than 5!

4Making changeCount up like a cashier›
I can count and make change up to $100. Number 10
Cashiers count up from the price to the amount paid: first to the next quarter or dollar, then to the next 5, 10 or 20.

Example $7.35 paid with a $20 bill

  1. $7.35 + 0.65 (2 quarters, 1 dime, 1 nickel) → $8.00
  2. $8.00 + 2.00 (a toonie) → $10.00
  3. $10.00 + 10.00 (a $10 bill) → $20.00
  4. Change: $0.65 + $2.00 + $10.00 = $12.65

Unit 4

Factors & Fractions

Packing boxes, sharing pizza, doubling a recipe, cutting wood to length: they all use multiples, factors and fractions. In this unit you'll find factors and primes, work with fractions and mixed numbers, and add and subtract fractions with the same denominator.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • find multiples of a number Number 4
  • find factors and tell prime numbers from composite numbers Number 4
  • show proper fractions, mixed numbers and equivalent fractions Number 5
  • compare and order fractions Number 6
  • add and subtract fractions with the same denominator Number 8

2Lessons

1MultiplesCounting by a number›
I can find multiples of a number. Number 4
Multiples are what you get when you count by a number (skip count): the multiples of 6 are 6, 12, 18, 24, 30…

Example Hot dog math

Hot dogs come in packs of 12. Buns come in packs of 8. How many of each do you need so none are left over?
  • Multiples of 12: 12, 24, 36, 48
  • Multiples of 8: 8, 16, 24, 32
  • 24 is the first number on both lists: 2 packs of hot dogs and 3 packs of buns.

Try it

🧭
Expedition: Pack Planner

Find 2 things at the grocery store that come in different pack sizes (hot dogs and buns, cups and plates). Figure out the smallest number of packs for no leftovers.

2Factors, primes and compositesNumbers that divide evenly›
I can find factors and tell prime numbers from composite numbers. Number 4
Factors divide into a number with no remainder. A prime number has exactly 2 factors: 1 and itself. A composite number has more than 2.

Example 1 Factors of 24

Find factor pairs: 1 × 24, 2 × 12, 3 × 8, 4 × 6. Factors of 24: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24, so 24 is composite.

Example 2 Arranging chairs

You have 24 chairs. You could make rows of 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12 or 24, because those are the factors. With 23 chairs (a prime), you can only make 1 long row or 23 rows of 1!

Try it

🧭
Expedition: Prime Hunter

Make a hundreds chart and cross out multiples of 2, 3, 5 and 7 (but not those numbers themselves). Circle what's left: those are the primes up to 100.

3Fractions and mixed numbersParts of a whole›
I can show proper fractions, mixed numbers and equivalent fractions. Number 5
In 3/4, the bottom (denominator) is how many equal parts, and the top (numerator) is how many you have. A mixed number like 2 1/2 is a whole number plus a fraction.
3/43 of 4 equal parts
1/2 = 2/4Equivalent fractions

Example Equivalent fractions

Multiply or divide the top and bottom by the same number: 1/2 = 2/4 = 4/8. 6/8 ÷ 2 = 3/4.

Example Mixed numbers

A recipe needs 2 1/2 cups of flour: 2 full cups plus half a cup. As halves, that's 5/2 cups.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Recipe Remix

Find a recipe with at least 3 fractions in it. Rewrite each amount as an equivalent fraction, then cut the recipe in half.

4Comparing fractionsWhich piece is bigger?›
I can compare and order fractions. Number 6
Same bottom number: the bigger top wins. Same top number: the smaller bottom wins, because the pieces are bigger.

Example 1

3/8 vs 5/8: same size pieces, and 5 pieces is more. 5/8 is greater.

Example 2 Pizza

1/4 of a pizza or 1/6 of the same pizza? Cutting into 4 makes bigger slices than cutting into 6. 1/4 is greater.

Try it

🧭
Expedition: Wrench Sorter

Find a set of wrenches or drill bits marked in inches (3/8, 1/2, 5/16…). Put them in order from smallest to largest using fractions. Ask an adult to check.

5Adding and subtracting fractionsSame denominator›
I can add and subtract fractions with the same denominator. Number 8
When the bottoms match, add or subtract the tops and keep the bottom the same. Simplify if you can.

Example 1 Adding

You walk 3/8 km to a friend's house, then 4/8 km to the store. 3/8 + 4/8 = 7/8 km.

Example 2 Subtracting and simplifying

A board is 5/6 m long. You cut off 2/6 m. 5/6 − 2/6 = 3/6 = 1/2 m left.

Example 3 More than one

3/4 + 3/4 = 6/4 = 1 2/4 = 1 1/2

Try it

🧭
Expedition: Kitchen Fractions

Help cook or bake something at home. Add up all the cups (or teaspoons) of ingredients using fractions. Show your work.

Percents & Discounts

Percents are everywhere: your phone battery, sale signs, the chance of snow, and your AvCash investments. In this unit you'll learn what a percent means, how it connects to decimals and fractions, and how to figure out a sale price in your head.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • explain that a percent means "out of 100" Number 11
  • change a percent to a decimal, and a decimal to a percent Number 11
  • match ½, ¼, ¾ and 1/10 to their decimals and percents Number 9
  • find 10%, 25% and 50% of an amount using mental math Number 11, 12
  • find a discount and a sale price Number 10
  • estimate first, then check if my answer makes sense Number 7, 12

2Lessons

1What is a percent?Percent means "out of 100"›
I can explain that a percent means "out of 100." Number 11
Per cent means per hundred. The sign % is a short way of writing "out of 100."

Example 1 A 100-square grid

35 out of 100 squares are shaded.
35 out of 100 = 35%
35% of the grid is shaded.

The whole grid is 100%. The part that is not shaded is 100 − 35 = 65%.

Example 2 Your phone battery

Your battery says 80%. That means 80 out of 100 parts are full.
  1. 100% would be completely full.
  2. 80% is 20 parts away from full.
  3. Your battery is mostly full.

Example 3 Chance of snow

The forecast says a 10% chance of snow. That is 10 out of 100, so snow is not very likely. A 90% chance would mean snow is very likely.

Try it

What percent of the grid is shaded?

Expedition: Percent Spotter

Find 5 percents in the real world (a food label, a sale sign, a weather app, a battery). Write down where you found each one and what it means. Bring your list to class.

2Percents and decimalsMoving the decimal two places›
I can change a percent to a decimal, and a decimal to a percent. Number 11
A percent is out of 100, so percent → decimal: divide by 100 (move the decimal point 2 places left). Decimal → percent: multiply by 100 (move it 2 places right).

Example 1 Percent to decimal

  1. Start with 45%. The decimal point is hiding after the 5: 45.
  2. Move it 2 places left: 0.45
  3. 45% = 0.45

Example 2 Watch out for small percents

  1. 8% → move 2 places left. There is only one digit, so add a zero to hold the place.
  2. 8% = 0.08 (not 0.8. That would be 80%!)

Example 3 Decimal to percent

  1. Start with 0.6.
  2. Move the decimal 2 places right. Add a zero: 60.
  3. 0.6 = 60%

Example 4 Money makes it easy

A loonie is $1.00, which is 100 cents. A quarter is $0.25: 25 cents out of 100. $0.25 is 25% of a dollar.

Try it

🧭
Expedition: Teach It Back

4Mental math: 10%, 25% and 50%Find a percent of an amount in your head›
I can find 10%, 25% and 50% of an amount using mental math. Number 11, 12
10% = move the decimal 1 place left. 50% = half. 25% = half of half. Build other percents from these.

Example 1 10% of $40

  1. $40 is $40.00. Move the decimal 1 place left: $4.00
  2. 10% of $40 = $4

Example 2 50% and 25% of $60

  1. 50% is half: $60 ÷ 2 = $30
  2. 25% is half of that: $30 ÷ 2 = $15
  3. 50% of $60 = $30, and 25% of $60 = $15

Example 3 Building other percents

  • 20% = 10% × 2 → 20% of $40 = $4 × 2 = $8
  • 5% = half of 10% → 5% of $40 = $4 ÷ 2 = $2
  • 30% = 10% × 3 → 30% of $40 = $4 × 3 = $12

5Discounts and sale pricesWhat will I actually pay?›
I can find a discount and a sale price. Number 10
The discount is how much you save. The sale price is what you pay.
sale price = regular price − discount

Example 1 A hoodie

$60.00 25% OFF
  1. Find the discount: 25% of $60 → half of half → $60 ÷ 2 = $30, $30 ÷ 2 = $15
  2. Subtract: $60 − $15 = $45
  3. You save $15 and pay $45.

Example 2 Shortcut: pay the rest

If something is 25% off, you pay the other 75% (because 100% − 25% = 75%).
On a calculator: 60 × 0.75 = 45 → $45, the same answer.

Example 3 Avalanche HQ sale day

40 AvCash 10% OFF
Homework pass
  1. 10% of 40 = 4
  2. 40 − 4 = 36 AvCash

6Does my answer make sense?Estimate, check, and add GST›
I can estimate first, then use a calculator and check if my answer makes sense. Number 7, 12
Round, estimate, then calculate. If your exact answer is far from your estimate, check your work.

Example 1 Estimate a sale

A jacket is $49.99, 30% off.
  1. Round: $49.99 is about $50.
  2. 30% of $50 → 10% is $5, so 30% is $15. Estimate: about $50 − $15 = $35.
  3. Calculator: 49.99 × 0.70 = 34.993 → $34.99
  4. $34.99 is close to $35, so it makes sense.

Example 2 GST in Alberta

In Alberta, most things you buy have 5% GST added at the till. 5% is half of 10%.
  1. Item costs $20. 10% of $20 = $2, so 5% = $1.
  2. Total: $20 + $1 = $21

Example 3 Spot the mistake

Jordan says 25% off $80 is $60.00, and Sam says it's $78.00.
  1. Estimate: 25% of $80 = $20, so about $60.
  2. Jordan makes sense. Sam probably took off $2 instead of 25%.

Try it: does it make sense?

Expedition: Receipt Check

With an adult, look at a real receipt. Estimate the GST (5%) on the subtotal, then compare it to what the receipt says. Was the store's number reasonable?

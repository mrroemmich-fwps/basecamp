Unit 1

Big Numbers & Rounding

Populations, house prices, the distance to the sun: some numbers are huge. In this unit you'll read and write numbers in the millions, use powers of 10 and scientific notation, round to the nearest unit, tenth and hundredth, and estimate to check your work.

2Powers of 10Expanded form›

A power of 10 is 10 multiplied by itself. The small raised number (the exponent) tells how many zeros: 103 = 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000.

3Scientific notationA short way to write huge numbers›
I can write big numbers in scientific notation. Number 4
Scientific notation writes a number as (a number from 1 to 9.99…) × (a power of 10). Scientists, engineers and calculators use it so they don't have to write all the zeros.

Example 1 Big → short

The sun is about 150,000,000 km from Earth. Move the decimal left until one digit is in front: 1.5. You moved it 8 places, so it's 1.5 × 108 km.

Example 2 Short → big

3.2 × 106: move the decimal 6 places right → 3,200,000.
📱 On many calculators, a big answer shows up as 3.2E6. The "E6" means × 106.

Find the place you're rounding to, then look at the digit just to its right. 5 or more: round up. 4 or less: keep it the same. Drop everything after.

5EstimatingDoes my answer make sense?›
I can estimate to check if an answer makes sense. Number 1, 9
Round first, then calculate. An estimate won't be exact, but it tells you if a calculator answer is way off, like when you hit a wrong key.

Example Ordering supplies

A school orders 312 notebooks at $3.85 each. Estimate: 300 × $4 = $1,200. The calculator says $1,201.20, which is close, so it makes sense. If it said $120.12, you'd know something went wrong.

Unit 2

Integers & Debt

A bank balance can drop below zero. So can the temperature, and so can a hockey player's plus/minus. In this unit you'll compare and order positive and negative numbers, track a bank account through deposits and withdrawals, and understand what it means to be in debt.

  • track a bank balance through deposits and withdrawals Number 5, 14
  • explain what debt and an overdraft are Number 14

1Integers in real lifeMoney in, money out›
I can use integers to describe money, temperature and elevation. Number 14
Integers are positive and negative whole numbers, plus zero. In money, positive means you have it or are getting it. Negative means you owe it or are spending it.

2Comparing and orderingNumber lines and thermometers›
I can compare and order integers on a number line or thermometer. Number 14
Numbers get bigger to the right on a number line (or higher up on a thermometer). So −2 is greater than −10, because −2 is closer to zero.

Example Who owes the most?

Three friends' balances: Ava −$45, Ben −$12, Cole $8. In order from least to greatest: −45, −12, 8. Ava owes the most.

4Debt and overdraftsGoing below zero›
I can explain what debt and an overdraft are. Number 14
Debt is money you owe. An overdraft happens when you spend more than is in your account and the balance goes negative. Banks usually charge a fee for it, which makes the hole deeper.

Example 1 Overdrawn

You have $40 and buy a $65 jacket with your debit card. 40 − 65 = −$25. With a $5 overdraft fee: −25 − 5 = −$30.

Example 2 Paying it back

You owe $90 on a phone. You pay $15 a month. −90 + 15 + 15 + 15 = −45. After 6 payments: −90 + 6 × 15 = $0, paid off.

Try it

5Finding the changeHow far apart are two integers?›
I can find the change between two integers. Number 14
To find the change, do end − start. When the numbers are on opposite sides of zero, count to zero first, then keep going.

Example 1 Temperature

A chinook takes Calgary from −15°C to 7°C. From −15 to 0 is 15°, then 0 to 7 is 7°. 15 + 7 = 22° warmer.

Example 2 Elevation

A diver goes from 3 m above the water (on the board) to 4 m below. 3 − (−4) = 7 m.

Unit 3

Decimals & Averages

Prices per kilogram, splitting a bill, cutting lumber, figuring out your average hours at work: decimals are everywhere on the job. In this unit you'll multiply and divide decimals, round sensibly, and find the mean by hand and in a spreadsheet.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • multiply decimals in everyday situations Number 5
  • divide decimals, including by 2-digit numbers Number 5
  • check an answer by estimating Number 2, 9
  • find the mean (average) of a set of numbers Number 11
  • use a spreadsheet to find totals and averages Statistics 8
🛒 Career connection: cashiers, butchers, carpenters, warehouse workers and office assistants use decimals and averages every shift.

2Lessons

1Multiplying decimalsPrice × amount›
I can multiply decimals in everyday situations. Number 5
Multiply like whole numbers, then count the decimal places in both numbers. Your answer has that many decimal places. On a calculator, just check that the answer is reasonable.

Example 1

2.4 × 1.5: 24 × 15 = 360. There are 2 decimal places in total, so 3.60 = 3.6. Check: about 2 × 2 = 4 ✓

Example 2 At the deli

Ham costs $2.39 per 100 g. You buy 350 g, which is 3.5 × 100 g. 2.39 × 3.5 = 8.365 → $8.37.

2Dividing decimalsSplitting, sharing and cutting›
I can divide decimals, including by 2-digit numbers. Number 5
Dividing tells you how many fit or how much each gets. If you're dividing by a decimal, multiply both numbers by 10 (or 100) until you're dividing by a whole number.

Example 1 Cutting a board

How many 0.4 m shelves come from a 2.8 m board? 2.8 ÷ 0.4 → 28 ÷ 4 = 7 shelves.

Example 2 Splitting a bill

14 players split a $175.70 team dinner. 175.70 ÷ 14 = 12.55 → $12.55 each. Check: 14 × 12.55 = 175.70 ✓

3Is it reasonable?Estimate, then check›
I can check an answer by estimating. Number 2, 9
Before you trust a calculator, round to friendly numbers and estimate. The most common mistake is the decimal point in the wrong place. An estimate catches it.

Example

A worker says 18 boxes at $4.95 is $891. Estimate: 20 × $5 = $100. $891 is way too big. The real answer is 18 × 4.95 = $89.10. The decimal was in the wrong place.

4The mean (average)Add them up, divide by how many›
I can find the mean (average) of a set of numbers. Number 11
The mean is one number that represents a whole group. mean = total ÷ how many.

Example Average tips

A server's tips over 5 shifts: $42, $58, $36, $71, $48. Total = $255. 255 ÷ 5 = $51 a shift.

Estimate first

All the tips are between $36 and $71, so the mean must be somewhere in between. $51 makes sense.

Unit 4

Factors & Fractions

Buying hot dogs and buns that match, packing snack bags, doubling a recipe, measuring with a tape: factors and fractions solve real problems. In this unit you'll find common multiples and factors, break numbers into primes, and add and subtract fractions, even when the denominators don't match.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • find the lowest common multiple (LCM) Number 3
  • find the greatest common factor (GCF) Number 3
  • write the prime factorization of a number Number 3
  • add and subtract mixed numbers with like denominators Number 6
  • use equivalent fractions to add and subtract with unlike denominators Number 7
👥 Side by side: Grade 8 is learning factors, primes and fractions with the same denominator in their Unit 4. You'll build on that.

2Lessons

1Lowest common multipleWhen do they match up?›
I can find the lowest common multiple (LCM). Number 3
The lowest common multiple is the smallest number that is in both times tables. List the multiples of each number until you find a match.

Example Hot dogs and buns

Hot dogs come in 12s, buns come in 8s.
Multiples of 12: 12, 24, 36, 48…
Multiples of 8: 8, 16, 24, 32…
LCM = 24. Buy 2 packs of hot dogs and 3 packs of buns.

Try it

🧭
Expedition: Shift Schedules

Two friends work part-time. One works every 3rd day and one every 4th day. Mark a one-month calendar to show the days they both work. Explain how the LCM helps you predict those days.

2Greatest common factorSharing with nothing left over›
I can find the greatest common factor (GCF). Number 3
The greatest common factor is the biggest number that divides evenly into both numbers. List the factors of each and find the biggest one they share.

Example Snack bags

You have 18 apples and 24 granola bars. You want identical bags with nothing left over.
Factors of 18: 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18
Factors of 24: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24
GCF = 6 bags, each with 3 apples and 4 bars.

Try it

🧭
Expedition: Care Packages

Plan care packages for a food bank or shelter using real amounts of 2 or 3 items. Use the GCF to find the most identical packages you can make.

3Prime factorizationBreaking a number into primes›
I can write the prime factorization of a number. Number 3
A prime has exactly 2 factors: 1 and itself (2, 3, 5, 7, 11…). Every other number can be written as primes multiplied together. A factor tree helps.

Example

Split 36 into any two factors (4 × 9). Keep splitting until every branch ends in a prime. 36 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3.

Try it

🧭
Expedition: Tree Challenge

Make factor trees for 60 two different ways (start with 6 × 10, then 4 × 15). Do you get the same primes? Show a classmate why.

4Mixed numbersAdding and subtracting with like denominators›
I can add and subtract mixed numbers with like denominators. Number 6
A mixed number has a whole part and a fraction part: 23/4. Add the wholes, add the fractions. If the fraction is more than 1, trade it for a whole.

Example 1 Lumber

23/4 ft + 13/4 ft: wholes 2 + 1 = 3, fractions 3/4 + 3/4 = 6/4 = 12/4. Total: 3 + 12/4 = 41/2 ft.

Example 2 Recipe

You have 31/3 cups of flour and use 12/3. You can't take 2/3 from 1/3, so trade: 31/3 = 24/3. 24/3 − 12/3 = 12/3 cups left.

Try it

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Measure It

Use a tape measure to find the length and width of 3 things at home to the nearest 1/4 inch. Add the length and width of each as mixed numbers.

5Unlike denominatorsMake them match first›
I can use equivalent fractions to add and subtract with unlike denominators. Number 7
You can only add pieces that are the same size. Use the LCM of the denominators as the new denominator, then rewrite each fraction.

Example Pizza night

You eat 1/3 of a pizza and your friend eats 1/4. How much is gone?
LCM of 3 and 4 is 12. 1/3 = 4/12 and 1/4 = 3/12.
4/12 + 3/12 = 7/12 of the pizza.
✅ Check with a picture: 1/3 + 1/4 should be a bit more than 1/2. 7/12 is a bit more than 6/12, so it makes sense.

Try it

🧭
Expedition: Recipe Remix

Find a recipe that uses at least 3 different fractions. Add up all the dry ingredients in cups using a common denominator.

Unit 5

Percents & Consumer Math

Every time you shop, eat out, compare prices or get a raise, you're using percents and rates. This unit gives you the tools to figure out what things really cost and to spot the best deal.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • convert between fractions, decimals and percents Number 8
  • calculate a percent of an amount and round to the nearest cent Number 2, 12
  • add GST and figure out a tip Number 12
  • find a sale price, even with "extra" discounts Number 12
  • use unit rates to compare prices and find the best buy Number 10, 13
  • find a percent increase or decrease and check if it makes sense Number 9, 12
👥 Side by side: Grade 8 is learning percents in their Unit 5 too. Grade 9s, you're the guides on this trail. Several Expeditions below ask you to coach a Grade 8 classmate.

2Lessons

1Fractions, decimals and percentsThree ways to write the same amount›
I can convert between fractions, decimals and percents. Number 8
Fraction → decimal: divide the top by the bottom. Decimal → percent: × 100. Percent → fraction: write it over 100 and simplify.

Example 1 Fraction to decimal to percent

  1. 3/8 → 3 ÷ 8 = 0.375
  2. 0.375 × 100 = 37.5%
  3. 3/8 = 0.375 = 37.5%

Example 2 Percent to fraction

  1. 40% = 40/100
  2. Divide top and bottom by 20: 40 ÷ 20 = 2, 100 ÷ 20 = 5
  3. 40% = 2/5

Example 3 On the job

A carpenter has used 7 of 20 boards. 7 ÷ 20 = 0.35, so 35% of the boards are used and 65% are left.

Try it

🧭
Expedition: Trail Guide

Coach a Grade 8 classmate through the benchmark table (½, ¼, ¾, 1/10). When they can do 5 in a row without help, you both check in with the teacher.

2Percent of an amountMultiply by the decimal›
I can calculate a percent of an amount and round to the nearest cent. Number 2, 12
To find a percent of an amount, change the percent to a decimal and multiply. Round money to 2 decimal places (the nearest cent).

Example 1 15% of $64

  1. 15% = 0.15
  2. 0.15 × 64 = 9.6
  3. $9.60 (money always gets 2 decimal places)

Example 2 Rounding to the nearest cent

  1. 8% of $36.50 → 0.08 × 36.50 = 2.92 $2.92
  2. 12% of $18.99 → 0.12 × 18.99 = 2.2788. Look at the 3rd decimal: 8 is 5 or more, so round up → $2.28

Example 3 Check with mental math

15% = 10% + 5%. 10% of $64 = $6.40, 5% = $3.20. Total = $9.60, the same as the calculator.

3GST and tipsWhat you pay on top of the price›
I can add GST and figure out a tip. Number 12
In Alberta, most purchases have 5% GST (Goods and Services Tax). Shortcut: total = price × 1.05. Tips at restaurants are usually 15% to 20% of the bill.

Example 1 GST two ways

A hoodie is $42.
  1. Long way: 5% of 42 = 0.05 × 42 = $2.10 → $42 + $2.10 = $44.10
  2. Shortcut: 42 × 1.05 = 44.1
  3. Total: $44.10

Example 2 A tip

The bill is $56.40 and you want to tip 15%.
  1. 0.15 × 56.40 = 8.46
  2. Mental check: 10% = $5.64, 5% ≈ $2.82, total ≈ $8.46 ✓
  3. Tip: $8.46. Many people round to $8.50.

Example 3 Working for tips

A server earns tips of 15% on $1,200 of sales in a shift: 0.15 × 1,200 = $180 in tips. Tips count as income and must be reported on taxes (that's coming up in CEFL).

Try it

4Sale prices and "extra" discountsPay the percent that's left›
I can find a sale price, even with "extra" discounts. Number 12
If something is 30% off, you pay 70%. sale price = price × (1 − discount)

Example 1 One discount

$125.00 30% OFF
  1. Pay 100% − 30% = 70% = 0.70
  2. 125 × 0.70 = 87.5
  3. $87.50

Example 2 "Take an extra 10% off the sale price"

The $125 boots are 30% off, then an extra 10% off.
  1. After 30% off: $87.50
  2. Extra 10% off the new price: 87.50 × 0.90 = 78.75
  3. $78.75, not the same as 40% off ($75). Stores know this!

Try it

5Unit rates and the best buyBigger isn't always cheaper›
I can use unit rates to compare prices and find the best buy. Number 10, 13
A rate compares two different things, like dollars per kilogram. A unit rate is the price for 1 unit: price ÷ amount. The lower unit price is the better buy.

Example 1 Cereal

A: 450 g for $4.29   B: 750 g for $6.49
  1. A: 4.29 ÷ 450 = $0.0095 per gram → about $0.95 per 100 g
  2. B: 6.49 ÷ 750 = $0.0087 per gram → about $0.87 per 100 g
  3. B is the better buy.

Example 2 Wages are rates too

Job A pays $340 for 20 hours. Job B pays $16.25 an hour.
  1. A: 340 ÷ 20 = $17.00 per hour
  2. Job A pays more per hour. In CEFL you'll compare benefits and hours too.

Try it

🧭
Expedition: Grocery Detective

At a store or on a grocery website, find 3 products that come in two sizes. Calculate the unit price of each. Was the bigger size always the better deal?

6Percent increase and decreaseRaises, price hikes and investments›
I can find a percent increase or decrease and check if it makes sense. Number 9, 12
percent change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100. Positive means it went up, negative means it went down.

Example 1 A raise

Your wage goes from $15.00 to $15.45 an hour.
  1. Change: 15.45 − 15.00 = 0.45
  2. 0.45 ÷ 15.00 = 0.03
  3. A 3% raise

Example 2 A price drop

A bike drops from $80 to $68.
  1. Change: 68 − 80 = −12
  2. −12 ÷ 80 = −0.15
  3. A 15% decrease

Example 3 Does it make sense?

A price goes from $200 to $230 and someone says it went up 30%. Check: 10% of 200 is 20, so 30% would be $60. The real change is $30, which is 15%, not 30%.

Try it

🏔️ Avalanche HQ connection: Track one of your investments for 3 Mondays. What was the percent change each week? Was it worth the risk?

Unit 6

Paycheques & Time

Your first job means time cards, hourly wages, maybe overtime, and a pay stub full of deductions. In this unit you'll add up hours and minutes, calculate gross pay, compare ways of getting paid, and figure out what you actually take home.

  • add and subtract hours and minutes Shape & Space 11
  • calculate pay from an hourly wage and a time card Number 5, 10
  • calculate overtime pay Number 5, 10
  • compare hourly wage, salary, commission and tips Number 10, 12

2Lessons

1Adding hours and minutes60 minutes make an hour›
I can add and subtract hours and minutes. Shape & Space 11
Add the hours, then add the minutes. Every 60 minutes trades for 1 hour. To use time in a pay calculation, change minutes to a decimal: 15 min = 0.25 h, 30 min = 0.5 h, 45 min = 0.75 h.

3Describing dataRange, median, mean, gaps and clusters›

The $42 is an outlier. It pulls the mean up, so the median better describes what most workers earn.

Scale: plan → real: multiply. Real → plan: divide. Use the same units.

Grid: (x, y) = across, then up. Negative x = left of zero (second quadrant).

Symmetry: a regular polygon has one line of symmetry per side. Reflection = flip · translation = slide · rotation = turn.

Unit 10

Data & Graphing

Employers track sales, coaches track stats, and you see graphs in the news every day. In this unit you'll read and build graphs, pick the right graph for the job, describe data with range, median and mean, use a spreadsheet, and talk about how likely things are.

1Reading graphsTitle, axes, trend›
I can read and interpret graphs and charts. Statistics 1
Read in this order: title (what is it about?), axes (what is measured, and in what units?), scale (what does each line stand for?), then the trend (going up, down or staying the same?).

Example Ice cream shop sales

Sales climb from April to a peak in July (about $9,000), then drop as the weather cools. The owner might hire summer staff for June to August.

3Describing dataRange, median, mean, gaps and clusters›
I can describe data with range, median, mean, gaps and clusters. Statistics 4–6
Range = biggest − smallest (how spread out). Median = the middle value when the data is in order. Mean = total ÷ how many. A cluster is where lots of values bunch together. A gap is where there are none. An outlier is a value far from the rest.

Example Hourly wages at a small business

$15, $15, $16, $16, $17, $18, $42 (the owner)
Range: 42 − 15 = $27. Median: the 4th value = $16. Mean: 139 ÷ 7 ≈ $19.86.
The $42 is an outlier. It pulls the mean up, so the median better describes what most workers earn.
4Conclusions and spreadsheetsWhat does the data say?›
I can make conclusions and predictions from data, using a spreadsheet. Statistics 3, 8
A good conclusion is backed up by the data. Ask: Is there enough data? Where did it come from? Could something else explain it? A spreadsheet can sort data, find =MEDIAN(), =AVERAGE(), =MAX() and =MIN(), and make a graph in a few clicks.

Example

A cafeteria sold 40 pizza slices on Monday and 12 on Tuesday. Someone says "students don't like pizza anymore." But Tuesday was a field trip day. The conclusion isn't supported. More days of data are needed.
5Probability wordsHow likely is it?›
I can use probability words to describe how likely something is. Statistics 7
Probability goes from 0 (impossible) to 1 or 100% (certain). In between: improbable (unlikely), equally likely (50/50), probable (likely). In the workplace you'll hear "best case" and "worst case" too.

Example

A die has 6 sides. Rolling a 4: 1 out of 6 ≈ 17%, so it's improbable. Rolling a number less than 6: 5 out of 6 ≈ 83%, so it's probable.

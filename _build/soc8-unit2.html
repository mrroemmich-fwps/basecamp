Japan: Isolation & Adaptation

For over 200 years, Japan closed its doors to most of the world. Then, in just a few decades, it changed faster than almost any country in history. In this unit you'll explore why Japan chose isolation, what happened when outsiders arrived, and how people keep traditions while the world changes around them.

1What is a worldview?The lens you see the world through›
I can explain what a worldview is and what shapes it. 8.1
A worldview is the way a person or society sees the world. It's built from beliefs (what you think is true), values (what you think matters) and knowledge (what you've learned).

What shapes a worldview?

  • Geography: where you live (mountains, ocean, prairie, city)
  • Time: when you live
  • Family, culture and religion
  • Contact with others: new people bring new ideas

Example Two students, two worldviews

A student who grew up on a farm near Lethbridge might value hard work, weather and land. A student who grew up downtown in Calgary might value transit, crowds and city jobs. Neither is wrong. Their surroundings shaped what they notice and care about.
2Japan's geographyAn island nation›
I can describe how Japan's geography affected its worldview. 8.1
Japan is a chain of islands, with four main islands, surrounded by ocean. Most of the land is mountains, so people crowded onto small areas of flat land near the coast.

How geography shaped Japan

  • The sea was a natural wall that made invasion hard, and a source of food.
  • Mountains meant little farmland, so rice farming and fishing were very important.
  • Being separate helped Japan build its own unique culture, language and art.

Example Compare with Alberta

Alberta has no ocean and lots of open prairie. Japan is about half Alberta's size but has many times more people. Space, food and safety depended on the land and sea in very different ways.
3Closing the doorsEdo Japan chooses isolation›
I can explain how and why Japan isolated itself. 8.1
From the early 1600s to 1868, Japan was ruled by a military leader called the shogun. In the 1630s the shogun closed Japan to almost all outsiders to keep control and protect Japanese ways.

Japan's social structure (top to bottom)

  1. Emperor: respected, but held little real power
  2. Shogun: the real ruler
  3. Daimyo: lords who controlled land
  4. Samurai: warriors who served the daimyo
  5. Peasants, artisans, merchants: farmers, craftspeople and traders

What isolation looked like

  • Most foreigners were not allowed in, and Japanese people could not leave.
  • Christianity was banned.
  • Only small amounts of trade were allowed, with Dutch and Chinese traders at one port, Nagasaki.
Result: about 200 years of peace and a strong Japanese culture, but Japan fell behind in technology.
4The black ships arriveContact in 1853›
I can describe what changed when the outside world arrived. 8.1
In 1853, American warships led by Commodore Matthew Perry sailed into Tokyo Bay. The Japanese called them the "black ships." Perry demanded that Japan open its ports to trade.

What happened next

  1. Japan's leaders saw the powerful steam-powered ships and cannons. Fighting back seemed impossible.
  2. Japan signed treaties opening ports to the United States, then to other countries.
  3. Many Japanese people felt the treaties were unfair, and anger at the shogun grew.
  4. In 1868 the shogun lost power, and the emperor was put back in charge. This was the start of the Meiji era.
5Rapid adaptationThe Meiji era›
I can explain how Japan adapted quickly while keeping traditions. 8.1
Japan's new leaders decided the best way to stay independent was to learn from the West, fast. They called it "rich country, strong army."
What changedWhat stayed
Railways, factories and telegraphs were builtThe Japanese language
Schools for all childrenShinto and Buddhist beliefs
A modern army and navy; the samurai class endedRespect for the emperor and family
Western-style clothes and governmentArts like tea ceremony, poetry and theatre

Opportunities and challenges

Opportunity: within about 40 years Japan became a world power. Challenge: the samurai lost their jobs and status, and many people struggled to keep old ways alive.
6Change and tradition here at homeFirst Nations, Métis and Inuit worldviews›
I can describe how First Nations, Métis and Inuit worldviews have been influenced by other worldviews. 8.1
Like Japan, First Nations, Métis and Inuit peoples experienced contact with a very different worldview. Unlike Japan, many changes were forced on them, not chosen.

How contact influenced worldviews

  • Language: residential schools forced many children to stop speaking their languages.
  • Spirituality: some ceremonies were banned by Canadian law for many years.
  • Land: treaties and settlement changed how people could live on and use the land.

Example Keeping traditions today

Today, communities across Alberta are bringing back languages like Cree and Blackfoot through school programs, elders and apps, and ceremonies are practised openly. Like Japan in the Meiji era, people are finding ways to keep their traditions strong while the world changes.

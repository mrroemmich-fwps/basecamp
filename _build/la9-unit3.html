Unit 3

Novel vs Film

The book is almost always different from the movie, and that's on purpose. In this unit you'll read a novel (or a set of stories), track plot, character and theme, learn how filmmakers tell stories with camera, sound and light, and decide which version does it better.

2Lessons

1Reading a longer textStrategies that work›
I can use reading strategies to stay on track with a longer text. 2.1.2
Novels are long, so use tools: predict before a chapter, question while reading, summarize each chapter in 1–2 sentences, keep a character list, and define new words in a personal glossary. Audiobooks are a great way to read along.
2Tracking the storyPlot, conflict and character arc›
I can analyze plot, conflict and character change across a novel. 2.2.2
A novel often has a main plot and subplots, and several conflicts at once. The main character's arc is how they change from beginning to end. Track it with evidence: a quote or event from the start, middle and end.

4Book vs. movieWhat changed, and why?›
I can compare a novel and its film adaptation. 1.1.2, 2.3.1
A book can show thoughts and take its time. A film has about two hours and must show everything. So filmmakers cut characters, combine scenes, change endings or add action. Ask: What changed? Why might they have changed it? Did it work?

Compare with a Venn diagram

Only in the book · in both · only in the film. Then pick the biggest change and explain its effect on the story.
5Writing a reviewOpinion + evidence›
I can write a review that supports my opinion with evidence. 2.2.1, 2.4.3
A good review gives a short summary (no spoilers!), a clear opinion, evidence (specific scenes, quotes or techniques) and a recommendation: who would enjoy it?

Example

"The film keeps the book's heart but loses its humour. The close-ups during the storm scene are powerful, but cutting the best friend's subplot makes the ending feel rushed. Read the book first, then watch it. 3.5/5."

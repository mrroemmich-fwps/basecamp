Stories & Scripts

Every good story has a hook, a problem and characters you care about. In this unit you'll map the plot of short stories, study how characters change, find the tricks authors use to build suspense, and turn a story into a script you perform.

2Lessons

1The plot mountainMapping a story›
I can map the parts of a plot and identify the main conflict. 2.2.2
The introduction sets up characters and setting. The initial incident starts the problem. Rising action builds tension. The climax is the turning point. Falling action wraps things up, and the resolution ends it. The conflict is the main problem: person vs. person, vs. self, vs. nature or vs. society.
2Characters who changeTraits, motives and choices›
I can describe how a character changes and why. 2.2.2
Traits are what a character is like (brave, selfish). Motivation is why they act. A dynamic character changes by the end; a static one stays the same. To prove a trait, find what the character says, does and thinks, and how others react to them.

Example

At the start, Jordan won't pass the puck to the new kid. After the new kid covers for Jordan's mistake, Jordan passes to him for the winning goal. Jordan changes from selfish to a team player, motivated by respect.
3Hooks and suspenseThe author's toolbox›
I can find hooks, foreshadowing, flashbacks and suspense. 2.3.2
TechniqueWhat it doesExample
HookGrabs the reader right away"The phone rang at 3 a.m., and nobody ever calls at 3 a.m."
ForeshadowingHints at what's coming"I should have checked the ice."
FlashbackJumps back to an earlier time"Two years earlier, Dad had warned me…"
SuspenseKeeps you worried or curiousShort sentences, a ticking clock, a cliffhanger
Surprise endingA twist you didn't expectThe "monster" was the family dog
4Setting and moodHow a story feels›
I can explain how setting and word choice create mood. 2.2.3, 2.3.3
Setting is when and where. Mood is the feeling the reader gets (creepy, cozy, tense). Authors build mood with setting details, word choice and figurative language like similes ("cold as steel") and metaphors ("the hallway was a tunnel").

Same place, two moods

Cozy: "Snow drifted past the window while the woodstove crackled and the kitchen smelled like bannock."
Creepy: "Snow hissed against the glass. The stove had gone cold, and something scraped across the porch." Same setting, different words, different mood.
5From story to scriptWrite it, then perform it›
I can write and perform a short script. 2.4.3, 4.3.1
A script tells a story through dialogue (what characters say) and stage directions (in brackets, in italics: what they do and how they say it). When you perform, use your voice (volume, tone, speed) and body (gestures, facial expressions).

Example

AVA: (whispering) Did you hear that?
LIAM: (rolls his eyes) It's just the furnace.
AVA: (grabbing his arm) Furnaces don't knock.

Stories & Scripts Cheat Sheet · Grade 8 Language Arts

Plot: introduction → initial incident → rising action → climax → falling action → resolution.

Conflict: person vs. person · vs. self · vs. nature · vs. society.

Character: traits (what they're like) · motivation (why) · dynamic (changes) or static (doesn't). Prove it with what they say, do and think.

Techniques: hook · foreshadowing (hints) · flashback (goes back) · suspense · surprise ending.

Mood: the feeling a story gives. Built with setting, word choice, similes (like/as) and metaphors.

Script: NAME: (stage direction) dialogue.

Grade 8 Science

Fluids, cells, light, machines and water: how science works at home, at work and in the community.

Unit 1 · On the trail now

Mix & Flow of Matter

Water, oil, paint, gasoline, blood and even air are all fluids. In this unit you'll learn how to handle fluids safely, what's really in a mixture, and why some fluids flow fast, some sink and some float.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • read WHMIS and household safety symbols and handle, store and dispose of fluids safely A1
  • tell the difference between pure substances, mixtures and solutions A2
  • explain solubility and concentration A2
  • describe viscosity and how temperature changes flow rate A3, A4
  • use density to explain why things sink or float A3
  • explain fluid pressure and the technology that uses it A3, A4
👥 Side by side: Grade 9 is learning WHMIS this unit too, in Matter & Chemical Change. Same symbols, same safety rules.

2Lessons

1Safety first: reading the symbolsWHMIS at work, household symbols at home›
I can read WHMIS and household safety symbols and handle, store and dispose of fluids safely. A1
Before you use any fluid, read the label. The symbol tells you what kind of danger it is.

At home Household product symbols

Cleaners, paint and fuels at home use these symbols. The frame tells you what is dangerous:

💀
PoisonHarmful if swallowed or breathed in. e.g., windshield washer fluid
🔥
FlammableCatches fire easily. e.g., gasoline
✋
CorrosiveBurns skin and eyes. e.g., oven cleaner
💥
Explosive containerCan burst if heated or punctured. e.g., spray cans

Octagon (stop-sign shape) = what's inside is dangerous. Triangle = the container is dangerous. The words CAUTION, WARNING and DANGER tell you how serious it is, from least to most.

At work and school WHMIS pictograms

WHMIS is the safety system used at workplaces across Canada. Its pictograms sit in a red diamond. A few you'll see often:

🔥
FlameFlammable
💀
Skull and crossbonesCan poison quickly
🧪
CorrosionBurns skin, eyes or metal
❗
Exclamation markCan irritate skin or eyes

The pictures here are a simple guide. Learn the real pictograms from the CCOHS link in Resources.

Safe handling Use, store, dispose

  • Use: read the label, wear goggles and gloves when told to, work with fresh air.
  • Store: in the original container, closed tight, away from heat and out of reach of kids.
  • Dispose: never pour chemicals down the drain. Take them to a household hazardous waste drop-off.
  • Never mix cleaners. Bleach mixed with ammonia makes a toxic gas.

2Pure substances, mixtures and solutionsWhat's really in there?›
I can tell the difference between pure substances, mixtures and solutions. A2
A pure substance has only one kind of particle. A mixture has two or more kinds.
Pure substanceOne kind of particle. e.g., pure water, sugar
Mechanical mixtureYou can see the different parts. e.g., trail mix, salad
SolutionMixed so evenly it looks like one thing. e.g., salt water, juice

Words to know

  • Solute: the thing that dissolves (salt, sugar, drink crystals).
  • Solvent: the thing it dissolves into (usually water).
  • Dissolve: break into particles so small you can't see them anymore.

Example Making a sports drink

Powder (solute) + water (solvent) → stir → the powder disappears and the colour spreads evenly. That's a solution. If you add ice cubes, you can see them, so the drink-plus-ice is a mechanical mixture.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Kitchen Sort

Find 9 things in your kitchen: 3 pure substances, 3 mechanical mixtures and 3 solutions. Take a photo or make a chart and explain each choice.

3Solubility and concentrationHow much will dissolve? How strong is it?›
I can explain solubility and concentration. A2
Solubility is how much solute can dissolve. Concentration is how much solute is in a certain amount of solution: concentration = solute ÷ volume.

Example 1 Which is stronger?

Cup A: 20 g of drink crystals in 250 mL. Cup B: 10 g in 250 mL.
  1. Same amount of water, but A has more crystals.
  2. Cup A is more concentrated (stronger taste, darker colour).

Example 2 Calculating concentration

30 g of salt in 100 mL of water: 30 ÷ 100 = 0.3 g/mL. Add 100 mL more water: 30 ÷ 200 = 0.15 g/mL. Adding water dilutes it.

Example 3 Saturated

Keep adding sugar to cold water and it eventually piles up on the bottom. The solution is saturated: no more will dissolve. Warm the water and more dissolves, because heat usually increases solubility.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Taste Test Lab

With an adult, make 3 cups of drink mix with different amounts of powder in the same amount of water. Rank them by concentration, then calculate g/mL for each.

4Viscosity and flow rateWhy ketchup is slow and water is fast›
I can describe viscosity and how temperature changes flow rate. A3, A4
Viscosity is a fluid's resistance to flowing. High viscosity = thick and slow (honey). Low viscosity = thin and fast (water). Flow rate is how fast it moves.

Example 1 Ranking fluids

From fastest flow to slowest: water → vegetable oil → dish soap → ketchup → honey

Example 2 Temperature

Cold honey barely moves. Warm it up and it pours easily. Heating a liquid usually lowers its viscosity, so it flows faster.

Example 3 On the job: motor oil

Motor oil labelled 5W-30 is made to stay thin in an Alberta winter ("W" = winter) so the engine can start, but not get too thin when the engine is hot. Mechanics choose oil by its viscosity.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Fluid Race

Race 4 kitchen fluids down a tilted plate and time each one. Make a bar graph of your results and bring it to class.

5Density and buoyancySink or float?›
I can use density to explain why things sink or float. A3
Density is how much mass is packed into a space: density = mass ÷ volume. Water's density is 1 g/mL. Less dense than water → floats. More dense → sinks.
Density columnLess dense liquids float on top

Example 1 Calculating density

A rock has a mass of 60 g and a volume of 20 mL. 60 ÷ 20 = 3 g/mL. That's more than 1, so it sinks.

Example 2 Ice

Ice is about 0.9 g/mL, a little less dense than water. That's why ice floats and lakes freeze from the top down, so fish survive the winter underneath.

Example 3 Buoyancy

Buoyancy is the upward push from a fluid. A life jacket is full of low-density foam, so it adds lots of buoyancy and keeps you floating.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Density Tower

With an adult, build a density tower in a clear glass using 3 or 4 liquids (honey, dish soap, water, oil). Drop in small objects and explain where each one stops.

6Pressure and fluid technologyTires, hoses, pumps and pipelines›
I can explain fluid pressure and the technology that uses it. A3, A4
Pressure is force pushing on an area: pressure = force ÷ area. Squeezing a fluid into a small space raises its pressure.

Example 1 Garden hose

Put your thumb over the end of a hose and the water sprays farther. A smaller opening means higher pressure.

Example 2 Tire pressure

Air pumped into a tire pushes outward on the inside. Low tire pressure in cold weather makes a car harder to steer and burns more gas. Tire pressure is measured in kPa or psi.

Example 3 On the job: pumps and pipelines

Alberta moves oil, natural gas and water through pipelines. Pumps raise the pressure to push fluids long distances. Thicker (more viscous) oil needs more pressure or heating to flow. Pipeline operators, welders and engineers all use this science every day.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Fluid Careers

Look up one Alberta career that works with fluids (pipefitter, heavy-duty mechanic, water treatment operator, paramedic) on alis.alberta.ca. Share 3 facts with the class: what they do, the training and the pay.

Unit 2

Fresh & Saltwater Systems

Almost all of Earth's water is salty, and only a tiny bit is fresh water we can use. In this unit you'll find out where water is, how it shapes the land, what lives in it, and how people keep it clean, from Alberta's rivers to the oceans.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • describe where Earth's water is and why fresh water matters E1
  • test water quality and explain how distillation makes fresh water E1
  • explain the water cycle and how water and ice shape the land E2
  • describe how living things are adapted to fresh and salt water E1, E3
  • explain how people affect water and how technology helps E4
💧 Career connection: water treatment operators, fisheries technicians, environmental monitors and plumbers all work to keep water safe.

2Lessons

1Water on EarthSalty, frozen or fresh?›
I can describe where Earth's water is and why fresh water matters. E1
About 97% of Earth's water is salt water in the oceans. Only about 3% is fresh, and most of that is frozen in glaciers and ice caps or deep underground. Less than 1% is easy for people to use.

Example Alberta's water

Most of southern Alberta's river water starts as snow and glacier melt in the Rocky Mountains. Rivers like the Bow and the North Saskatchewan carry it to towns and farms. When glaciers shrink, there is less water in late summer.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Where Does My Water Come From?

Find out where your town or farm gets its water (a river, a lake, a well). Draw a map from the source to your tap.

2Water qualityTesting and cleaning water›
I can test water quality and explain how distillation makes fresh water. E1
Clear water isn't always clean. Water quality tests check pH (acid or base), dissolved oxygen (for fish), hardness (minerals) and bacteria. Distillation boils water and collects the steam, leaving salt and dirt behind.

Example Boil-water advisory

If tests find harmful bacteria in a town's water, health officials issue a boil-water advisory. Boiling kills bacteria, but it doesn't remove salt or chemicals. That takes distillation or special filters.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Water Tester

With your teacher's kit or test strips, test tap water, rain or snowmelt, and pond or slough water. Compare the pH and hardness in a chart.

3The water cycle and the landErosion, deposition and glaciers›
I can explain the water cycle and how water and ice shape the land. E2
Water moves in a cycle: evaporation → condensation (clouds) → precipitation (rain, snow) → runoff and groundwater → back to the ocean. Along the way, moving water and ice erode (wear away) land and deposit (drop) it somewhere else.

Example 1 Rivers

Fast water in the mountains carves deep valleys. When a river slows down on the prairie, it drops sand and mud, building sandbars and flat floodplains.

Example 2 The Columbia Icefield

The Athabasca Glacier, near the Icefields Parkway, is part of the Columbia Icefield. Glaciers grind rock as they move and leave behind piles of rock called moraines. The Athabasca Glacier has shrunk more than 1.5 km in the last 125 years.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Water Cycle in a Bag

Put a little water in a sealed zip bag, tape it to a sunny window and watch it for a week. Draw and label each step of the water cycle you see.

4Life in the waterAdaptations and populations›
I can describe how living things are adapted to fresh and salt water. E1, E3
An adaptation is a feature that helps a living thing survive where it lives. Water animals need ways to breathe, move, find food and handle the salt (or lack of it).
Living thingAdaptationHow it helps
FishGillsTake oxygen out of the water
BeaverWebbed back feet, flat tailSwims and steers well
Water striderLong, light legsWalks on the water's surface
SalmonBody adjusts to salt and fresh waterLives in the ocean, returns to rivers to spawn
CattailsAir spaces in stemsGet air to roots in mud

Example Populations change

Fish numbers in a lake rise and fall with the seasons (spawning, winter), over years (fishing, drought) and when water quality changes. If oxygen drops in a hot summer, fish can die off.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Pond Life

Look up (or visit with an adult) 3 animals that live in an Alberta pond, slough or lake. Draw each one and label one adaptation that helps it survive.

5People and waterPollution and solutions›
I can explain how people affect water and how technology helps. E4
We use water to drink, farm, make things and have fun. Pollutants from homes, farms and industry can harm water life. Technology like water treatment plants helps, but some problems need people to change what they do.
SourcePollutantWhat helps
HomesSoaps, oil, chemicals down the drainWastewater treatment, hazardous waste drop-off
FarmsFertilizer and manure runoffBuffer strips of plants along creeks
Towns and roadsRoad salt, litter, motor oilStorm ponds, cleanups
IndustryChemicals and warm waterLaws, monitoring, recycling water

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Water Saver

Track how much water your household uses in one day (showers, dishes, laundry). Make a plan with 3 ways your family could save water, and try one for a week.

Unit 3

Mechanical Systems

Bikes, wrenches, cranes, can openers and even your own arm are machines. In this unit you'll see how simple machines make work easier, how gears and linkages pass force along, and how to judge whether a machine does its job well.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • describe how machines have changed over time D1
  • identify simple machines in everyday and traditional tools D2
  • explain levers and find mechanical advantage D2, D3
  • explain how gears and linkages transfer force, and measure work in joules D2, D3
  • evaluate a machine for function, safety and efficiency D4
🔧 Career connection: heavy-duty mechanics, millwrights, bike techs, machinists and agricultural equipment technicians work with these systems every day.

2Lessons

1Machines over timeFrom muscle to motors›
I can describe how machines have changed over time. D1
People have always needed to lift, move and cut things. The need stays the same, but the machines and their energy sources change: muscles → animals → water and wind → engines → electricity and batteries.
NeedLong agoToday
Moving loadsTravois pulled by dogs or horsesTrucks, conveyor belts
Lifting waterPersian wheel (buckets on a wheel turned by animals)Electric water pumps
Grinding grainWater wheel turning a mill stoneElectric mills
FlyingTrial-and-error glidersJets designed with science and computers

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Then and Now

Interview a grandparent or older neighbour about a machine they used growing up (a wringer washer, a hand pump, a crank phone). Compare it with what we use now.

2Simple machinesSix ways to make work easier›
I can identify simple machines in everyday and traditional tools. D2
There are six simple machines. Most tools are one of these, or several put together (a compound machine).
Simple machineEveryday example
LeverCrowbar, seesaw, hockey stick
Wheel and axleDoorknob, steering wheel
PulleyFlagpole, window blinds
Inclined planeWheelchair ramp, loading ramp
WedgeAxe, knife, doorstop
ScrewJar lid, bolt, drill bit

Example A traditional travois

A travois is two long poles with a platform between them. One end rests on a dog or horse and the other drags on the ground, so the poles work like levers. The animal can pull a load much heavier than it could carry.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Machine Hunt

Find all six simple machines at home or in the school shop. Photograph or sketch each one and label it.

3Levers and mechanical advantageLift more with less›
I can explain levers and find mechanical advantage. D2, D3
A lever has a fulcrum (the pivot), a load (what you move) and an effort (your push). Mechanical advantage (MA) tells how many times a machine multiplies your force: MA = load ÷ effort. Moving the fulcrum closer to the load makes lifting easier.

Example A crowbar

You push with 50 N and lift a 400 N crate. MA = 400 ÷ 50 = 8. The crowbar makes your push 8 times stronger, but you have to push your end much farther.

Try it

🧭
Expedition: Lever Lab

Build a lever with a ruler and a pencil as the fulcrum. Lift a stack of coins with coins on the other end. Move the fulcrum and record how many coins it takes each time.

4Gears, linkages and workPassing force along›
I can explain how gears and linkages transfer force, and measure work in joules. D2, D3
Gears pass turning force from one shaft to another. A small gear turning a big gear gives more force but less speed. A big gear turning a small gear gives more speed but less force. Work is force moving something a distance: work (J) = force (N) × distance (m).

Example 1 Bike gears

Climbing a hill, you shift so the chain is on a small front gear and a big back gear. Pedalling is easier, but you go slower. On flat ground, you shift the other way for more speed.

Example 2 Work

You push a wheelbarrow with 120 N for 5 m. Work = 120 × 5 = 600 J.

Try it

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Bike Tech

Look at a bike (yours or a shop's). Count the teeth on the front and back gears. Explain which combination is best for hills and which is best for speed.

5Judging a machineDoes it do the job well?›
I can evaluate a machine for function, safety and efficiency. D4
Engineers judge a design with criteria: Does it work? Is it reliable? Is it safe? Is it efficient (little energy wasted)? What's its effect on the environment? What does it cost?

Example Snow removal

Snow shovelGas snowblowerElectric snowblower
SpeedSlowFastMedium
CostLowHighMedium
EnvironmentNo fuelBurns gas, noisyUses electricity, quieter
SafetyBack strainMoving bladesMoving blades, cord
The "best" choice depends on which criteria matter most for the size of the driveway and the person using it.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Build and Test

Design and build a machine that lifts a small load (a pulley, crane or catapult) from recycled materials. Test it, then rate it on function, safety and efficiency.

Unit 4

Cells & Systems

Every living thing is made of cells, from a single-celled amoeba to a grizzly bear. In this unit you'll use a microscope, compare plant and animal cells, see how cells build tissues, organs and systems, and learn how to keep your own body systems healthy.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • describe what makes something an organism and how it works as a system B1
  • use a microscope and compare plant and animal cells B2
  • explain how cells, tissues, organs and systems are organized B2
  • describe the respiratory, circulatory and digestive systems B3
  • explain how choices and research affect health B3, B4
🩺 Career connection: nurses, lab technicians, paramedics, personal trainers and veterinary assistants use this science every day.

2Lessons

1Living things as systemsWhat is an organism?›
I can describe what makes something an organism and how it works as a system. B1
An organism is any living thing. All organisms take in food and energy, get rid of waste, grow, respond to their surroundings and reproduce. A system is a group of parts working together, and every organism is a system.

Example Same need, different ways

All organisms need food. A moose grazes on plants. A spider builds a web. A plant makes its own food from sunlight. A mushroom breaks down dead leaves. Same job, different tools.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Food Finders

Pick 4 Alberta animals or plants. Make a chart showing how each one gets food, water and oxygen.

2Cells and microscopesPlant vs. animal cells›
I can use a microscope and compare plant and animal cells. B2
The cell is the basic unit of life. Some organisms are one cell (an amoeba). Others have trillions (you). Plant cells have a stiff cell wall and green chloroplasts. Animal cells have only a flexible cell membrane.
Plant cellCell wall (box shape), chloroplasts, nucleus
Animal cellCell membrane only (rounded), nucleus

Microscope tips

  • Carry it with two hands: one on the arm, one under the base.
  • Start on the lowest power and focus with the coarse knob.
  • Switch to higher power and use only the fine focus.
  • Draw what you see with a pencil, label it, and write the magnification.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Cell Model

Build a model of a plant or animal cell with food or craft supplies. Label every part and explain what it does.

3Cells to systemsBuilding a body›
I can explain how cells, tissues, organs and systems are organized. B2
Cells of the same kind group into tissues. Tissues build organs. Organs work together in organ systems. Systems together make an organism.
LevelExample
CellMuscle cell
TissueHeart muscle
OrganHeart

🧭

I can describe the respiratory, circulatory and digestive systems. B3
SystemMain partsJob
RespiratoryNose, windpipe, lungsTake in oxygen, get rid of carbon dioxide

Example Systems working together

When you run, your muscles need more oxygen. You breathe faster (respiratory) and your heart beats faster (circulatory) to deliver it. Systems respond together.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Heart Rate Lab

Take your pulse for 15 seconds and multiply by 4. Do it resting, after walking and after 1 minute of jumping jacks. Graph your results.

5Keeping systems healthyChoices and research›
I can explain how choices and research affect health. B3, B4
Exercise, sleep, food choices and clean air keep body systems working. Illness, aging, smoking and vaping and poor air quality (like wildfire smoke) can harm them. Scientists do research that leads to new medicines, vaccines and treatments.

Example Wildfire smoke

On smoky days, tiny particles get deep into the lungs. Checking the Air Quality Health Index (AQHI) and staying indoors when it's high protects your respiratory system.

Example Research that changed lives

Insulin was first used to treat diabetes in 1922 by a team at the University of Toronto. Before that, type 1 diabetes was almost always fatal.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Healthy Habit Challenge

Pick one habit that helps a body system (more water, 30 minutes of activity, earlier bedtime). Track it for 2 weeks and report how you felt.

Unit 5

Light & Optical Systems

Phone cameras, glasses, microscopes, headlights and your own eyes all control light. In this unit you'll learn what light is, how it bounces and bends, how lenses make images, and how imaging technology is used at work and in medicine.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • describe what light is, how it travels and where it comes from C1
  • explain reflection and use mirrors C2
  • explain refraction, transmission and absorption C2
  • describe how convex and concave lenses form images C1, C3
  • compare the eye with a camera and describe imaging technologies C3
📷 Career connection: optometrists, opticians, photographers, X-ray and ultrasound technologists, and lighting technicians all work with light.

2Lessons

1What is light?Sources and how light travels›
I can describe what light is, how it travels and where it comes from. C1
Light is a form of energy. It travels in straight lines (rays) and is incredibly fast: about 300,000 km every second. Light comes from natural sources (the sun, fire, fireflies) and artificial sources (light bulbs, screens).

Example Changing light bulbs

Your grandparents mostly used incandescent bulbs, which waste most of their energy as heat. Today, LED bulbs give the same light with much less electricity and last many years longer.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Lighting Then and Now

Ask an older relative how homes were lit when they were young. Count the light sources in your home now and sort them as incandescent, fluorescent or LED.

2ReflectionBouncing light off mirrors›
I can explain reflection and use mirrors. C2
Reflection is light bouncing off a surface. On a smooth mirror, light bounces off at the same angle it came in. Rough surfaces scatter light in all directions.

Example On the road

A car's side mirror is curved outward so you can see more of the road. That's why it says "objects in mirror are closer than they appear."

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Periscope Builder

Build a periscope from a milk carton and two small mirrors. Use it to see over a table. Draw the path the light takes.

3Refraction and materialsBending, passing through, soaking up›
I can explain refraction, transmission and absorption. C2
Refraction is light bending as it passes from one material into another (air to water). Materials can be transparent (let light through, like clear glass), translucent (let some through, like frosted glass) or opaque (block it, like wood). Dark materials absorb more light and warm up.

Example The bent straw

A straw in a glass of water looks broken at the surface. Light from the straw bends as it leaves the water, so your eyes see it in the wrong place.

Example Rainbows

Sunlight is made of all the colours. Raindrops refract each colour a slightly different amount, spreading white light into a rainbow.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Material Sort

Test 10 materials at home with a flashlight. Sort them as transparent, translucent or opaque in a chart.

4LensesConvex and concave›
I can describe how convex and concave lenses form images. C1, C3
A convex lens is thicker in the middle. It brings light rays together and can make things look bigger (magnifying glass, microscope, camera). A concave lens is thinner in the middle. It spreads rays apart and makes things look smaller.

Example Glasses

People who are far-sighted (blurry up close) wear convex lenses. People who are near-sighted (blurry far away) wear concave lenses.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Water Lens

Put a drop of water on clear plastic wrap over newspaper print. What happens to the letters? Explain what kind of lens the drop makes.

I can compare the eye with a camera and describe imaging technologies. C3
JobEyeCamera
Let light inPupilAperture

Some technology "sees" what our eyes can't. Infrared cameras show heat, which helps firefighters find people in smoke. X-rays pass through skin to show bones. Laser eye surgery reshapes the cornea so some people don't need glasses.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Eye Exam Explainer

Find out what happens at an eye exam (ask an optometrist, a family member or look it up). Make a short guide for a classmate who has never had one.

Safety symbols at home: the symbol shows the type of danger (poison, flammable, corrosive, explosive). Octagon = contents are dangerous. Triangle = container is dangerous. Caution → Warning → Danger (least to most serious).

Fresh & Saltwater Systems Cheat Sheet · Grade 8 Science

Earth's water: about 97% salt water, 3% fresh. Most fresh water is frozen in glaciers and ice caps.

Water cycle: evaporation → condensation → precipitation → runoff and groundwater → back to the ocean.

WordMeaning
ErosionWearing away land and carrying it off
DepositionDropping sand, mud or rock in a new place
DistillationBoiling water and collecting the steam to remove salt
AdaptationA feature that helps a living thing survive
MoraineA pile of rock left behind by a glacier

Water quality tests: pH, dissolved oxygen, hardness, bacteria.

Protect water: never pour chemicals or oil down drains, recycle used oil, keep plants along creeks.

Mechanical Systems Cheat Sheet · Grade 8 Science

Six simple machines: lever · wheel and axle · pulley · inclined plane · wedge · screw. A compound machine combines several.

Lever parts: fulcrum (pivot) · load (what moves) · effort (your push). Fulcrum closer to the load = easier lift.

Mechanical advantage: MA = load ÷ effort. 400 N ÷ 50 N = 8.

Gears: small turning big = more force, less speed. Big turning small = more speed, less force.

Work: joules (J) = force (N) × distance (m). 120 N × 5 m = 600 J.

Judging a design: function · reliability · safety · efficiency · environment · cost.

Light & Optical Systems Cheat Sheet · Grade 8 Science

Light: energy that travels in straight lines at about 300,000 km/s. Natural sources (sun, fire) and artificial sources (bulbs, screens).

Reflection: bouncing. Angle in = angle out. Refraction: bending when light changes materials.

Transparent (all light through) · translucent (some light) · opaque (no light). Dark colours absorb more light and heat up.

Convex lens: thicker in the middle, brings light together, magnifies. Concave lens: thinner in the middle, spreads light apart.

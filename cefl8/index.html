Unit 1

Skills, Identity & the World of Work

Jobs change. New technology, the economy and the world keep reshaping what work looks like. In this unit you'll learn how the labour market moves, which skills travel with you to any job, how to build career resilience, and how to set career goals that can bend without breaking.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • explain what changes the labour market, including technology 8.1
  • identify and rank transferable and employability skills 8.1, 8.3
  • explain career resilience, human capital and growth mindset 8.1, 8.3
  • research who is represented in different careers 8.3
  • build a career plan with short- and long-term goals 8.1
👥 Side by side: CEFL 9 is studying automation and growing industries. Your work here sets that up.

2Lessons

1A changing labour marketWhy jobs come and go›
I can explain what changes the labour market, including technology. 8.1
The labour market is where workers and employers meet. It keeps changing because of economic cycles (booms and slowdowns, like oil prices in Alberta), technology (new tools replace some tasks and create new jobs) and social change (an aging population needs more health-care workers).
Technology changePositiveNegative
Self-checkoutFaster lines, new tech-support jobsFewer cashier jobs
Drones on farmsSpot crop problems earlySome field-scouting work disappears

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Job Then and Now

Interview an adult about a job they had that has changed (or disappeared) because of technology. What new skills did they need?

2Skills that travelTransferable and employability skills›
I can identify and rank transferable and employability skills. 8.1, 8.3
Transferable skills work in almost any job: communication, teamwork, problem solving, critical thinking, using technology and time management. Employers often rank these as high as job-specific skills. You build them through school, sports, volunteering, chores and part-time work.

Example

Coaching a kids' soccer team builds communication, leadership, patience and time management. Those skills transfer to a job at a store, a daycare or a construction site.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Skill Ranking

List 8 skills you have. Rank them from strongest to weakest and give a real example for your top 3.

3Career resilienceHuman capital and growth mindset›
I can explain career resilience, human capital and growth mindset. 8.1, 8.3
Job security means staying in one job a long time. Career security means you can find new work quickly when things change. You build career resilience through continuous learning, networking and agility. Your human capital is the value of your education, skills, knowledge and experience combined. A growth mindset says "I can't do it yet."

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Learn Something New

Learn a new skill in 2 weeks (a coding basic, a recipe, a tool, a language phrase set). Show what you learned and how it adds to your human capital.

4Who does this job?Representation in careers›
I can research who is represented in different careers. 8.3
Some careers have far more of one group than another (for example, few women in some trades, few men in some health-care jobs). Representation matters: when you see people like you in a career, it's easier to picture yourself there. Many programs now encourage under-represented groups into these fields.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Role Model Profile

Find someone from a group that's under-represented in their career (in your community or online). Make a profile of their path and the barriers they overcame.

5Career goalsMilestones, not a straight line›
I can build a career plan with short- and long-term goals. 8.1
Clear goals guide your choices about school and skills. Short-term goals (months to a year) are steps toward long-term goals (years). Keep goals flexible: goals that are too rigid can cause stress if things change.

Example

Long-term: work as a veterinary technologist. Milestones: volunteer at an animal shelter (this year) → take Biology in Grade 10 → get a part-time job at a pet store → apply to a vet tech program. Backup: animal care attendant or groomer.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Milestone Map

Draw a career path map with one long-term goal and at least 4 milestones. Add one backup route.

Unit 2 · On the trail now

Getting Hired: Job Offers & Pay

Two jobs offer you work. Which one is really the better deal? In this unit you'll learn to read a job offer, turn an hourly wage into a yearly amount, add up total compensation, look past the money, and start conversations that open doors.

  • weigh non-salary factors like hours, conditions and promotion 8.4
  • compare two job offers and choose the one that fits my goals 8.4
  • start a conversation with someone about their career 8.2

2Lessons

1Reading a job offerWhat's really in there?›
I can read a job offer and find the key information. 8.4
A job offer is what an employer gives you when they want to hire you. Before you say yes, find the pay, hours, start date, duties and benefits.

Example A job offer letter

Dear Taylor, We are happy to offer you the position of Kitchen Helper at Trailside Café. Pay: $16.50 per hour · Hours: about 20 hours a week (part-time) Start date: Monday, June 29 · Duties: dishwashing, food prep, cleaning Extras: free staff meal each shift, 10% employee discount Please reply by Friday to accept.
  • Pay: $16.50/hour
  • Hours: about 20 a week, so part-time
  • Extras: free meal, discount. These have real value too!

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Offer Detective

Ask an adult you trust what their first job offer included. Fill out a chart with the pay, hours, duties and extras.

2Hourly, weekly and yearly payWhat does $16.50 an hour really mean?›
I can turn an hourly wage into weekly and yearly pay. 8.4
weekly pay = hourly wage × hours per week
yearly pay = weekly pay × 52 (there are 52 weeks in a year)

Example 1 Part-time

  1. $16.50 × 20 hours = $330 a week
  2. $330 × 52 = $17,160 a year

Example 2 Full-time

  1. $20 × 40 hours = $800 a week
  2. $800 × 52 = $41,600 a year

This is gross pay, before taxes and deductions come off. You'll learn about that in Unit 3.

3Total compensationPay + bonuses + benefits›
I can calculate total compensation: pay + bonuses + benefits. 8.4
Total compensation is everything a job gives you, not just your paycheque.
total compensation = pay + bonuses + allowances + value of benefits
TypeWhat it isExample
BonusExtra money, often once a year$1,000 holiday bonus
AllowanceMoney for a work cost$50 a month for your phone
BenefitsThings paid for by the employerDental, glasses, prescriptions
Paid vacationTime off that is still paid2 weeks a year

Example

Salary $38,000 + bonus $1,500 + phone allowance $600 ($50 × 12) + health benefits worth $2,400 = $42,500 total compensation

4More than moneyNon-salary factors›
I can weigh non-salary factors like hours, conditions and promotion. 8.4
The highest-paying job isn't always the best one. Non-salary factors can matter just as much.

Things to think about

  • Hours: days, nights, weekends? Do they fit school, sports and family?
  • Conditions: indoors or outdoors, safe, friendly team?
  • Travel: how far, and how will you get there?
  • Promotion: can you move up and earn more later?
  • Learning: will you gain skills for your future career?

Example

Job A pays $1 an hour more but is a 45-minute bus ride each way and every weekend. Job B is a 5-minute walk, after school only, and trains you to become a shift lead. Many people would pick Job B.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: My Top 3

Rank the 6 non-salary factors from most to least important to you, and explain your top 3 to a family member. Did their list match yours?

5Comparing two job offersPut it all together›
I can compare two job offers and choose the one that fits my goals. 8.4
Compare offers side by side: total compensation first, then non-salary factors. The best job is the one that fits your goals and your life.

Example Two summer jobs, 40 hours a week for 8 weeks

The grocery store pays a bit more in total and could last past summer. But if you love being outside, the golf course might be the better fit.

6NetworkingTalking to people about careers›
I can start a conversation with someone about their career. 8.2
Networking is building relationships with people who can share information and ideas about careers. It starts with simple, polite questions.

Conversation starters

  • "How did you get started in your job?"
  • "What does a normal day look like?"
  • "What training or school did you need?"
  • "What do you wish you'd known at my age?"

Example

At a family gathering, Sam asks a cousin who's an electrician, "What did you have to do to become an apprentice?" The cousin explains the high school courses that helped and offers to show Sam a job site. One question opened a door.

Unit 3

Paycheques, Taxes & Worker Rights

Your first paycheque will be smaller than you expect, and that's because of taxes and deductions. In this unit you'll read a pay stub, learn how tax brackets work, compare progressive, regressive and flat taxes, see what taxes pay for, and know your rights as a young worker in Alberta.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • explain gross pay, net pay and mandatory deductions 8.4 (builds on Gr 7)
  • calculate income tax using tax brackets 8.4
  • compare progressive, regressive and flat tax systems 8.4
  • explain the social contract and what taxes pay for 8.4
  • describe my rights and responsibilities as a young worker 8.3, 8.4
👥 Side by side: Social 9 is looking at why we pay taxes. Math 9 Unit 6 practises net pay with real numbers.

2Lessons

1Gross vs. netWhere did my money go?›
I can explain gross pay, net pay and mandatory deductions. 8.4 (builds on Gr 7)
Gross pay is what you earn. Mandatory deductions come off before you're paid: income tax, CPP (Canada Pension Plan, for retirement) and EI (Employment Insurance, if you lose your job). Some jobs also take union dues or benefits. What's left is net pay.

Example

Gross $600 − income tax $48 − CPP $30 − EI $10 = net $512.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: First Paycheque

Ask a family member how they felt about their first paycheque. Was it more or less than they expected?

2Tax bracketsDifferent rates for different parts›
I can calculate income tax using tax brackets. 8.4
Canada uses tax brackets: different portions of your income are taxed at different rates. Moving into a higher bracket only raises the tax on the dollars above that line, not on all your income. (The numbers below are made up to keep the math simple.)

Example Made-up brackets: 15% up to $50,000, 25% above

Income $70,000: first 50,000 × 0.15 = $7,500. Next 20,000 × 0.25 = $5,000. Total tax $12,500, which is about 18% of the whole income.

🧭
Expedition: Bracket Myth Buster

Many people think a raise into a higher bracket means you take home less. Make a short explainer (poster or video) showing why that's not true.

SystemHow it worksExample
ProgressiveHigher incomes pay a higher rateCanada's income tax
FlatEveryone pays the same rateA single rate for all incomes
RegressiveLower incomes pay a bigger share of what they earnA $100 fee for everyone: a big bite from a small income, a small bite from a large one

4What taxes pay forThe social contract›
I can explain the social contract and what taxes pay for. 8.4
The social contract is the idea that we each pay taxes so everyone can share public services: health care, schools, roads, police, firefighters, parks and programs like the Canada Child Benefit. Your contribution helps others, and theirs helps you.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Tax Spotter

For one day, list every public service you use (bus, school, streetlights, library). Estimate what your day would cost if you paid for each yourself.

5Young worker rightsSafe and fair work›
I can describe my rights and responsibilities as a young worker. 8.3, 8.4
In Alberta, Employment Standards set rules for pay, hours, breaks and young workers (there are limits on the jobs and hours for teens). Occupational Health and Safety gives every worker three rights: the right to know about hazards, the right to participate in safety, and the right to refuse unsafe work.

Your responsibilities

  • Follow safety rules and wear protective equipment
  • Report hazards and injuries
  • Show up on time and do your job well

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Safety Poster

Make a poster for teen workers showing the 3 safety rights, with a real example of each.

Unit 4

Spending, Saving & Interest

Money in, money out, and money that grows. In this unit you'll separate fixed and variable costs, build a budget, learn how simple and compound interest work for you (or against you), and compare saving with investing and the risks that come with it.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • tell fixed costs from variable costs 8.5
  • create a budget and adjust it to reach a goal 8.5
  • calculate simple interest 8.5
  • calculate compound interest and explain why time matters 8.5
  • compare saving and investing, including risk and return 8.5
🏔️ Avalanche HQ connection: Your AvCash investments are a safe place to practise risk and return before it's real money.

2Lessons

1Fixed and variable costsWhat changes, what doesn't›
I can tell fixed costs from variable costs. 8.5
A cost is the time, money or resources needed to get or keep something. Fixed costs stay the same each month (rent, insurance, a phone plan). Variable costs change with your choices (food, gas, entertainment). Variable costs are where you have the most control.

Try it

🧭
Expedition: Cost Detective

With a family member, list 10 household costs. Sort them into fixed and variable, and circle the one that would be easiest to lower.

2Building a budgetPlan before you spend›
I can create a budget and adjust it to reach a goal. 8.5
A budget plans your money: income − fixed costs − variable costs − savings = 0. Pay yourself first by putting savings in before spending. To reach a goal faster, adjust variable costs.

Example Monthly teen budget

Income (part-time job)$400
Phone (fixed)−$45
Savings for a laptop−$150
Food and fun (variable)−$205
Left$0
Laptop costs $900: 900 ÷ 150 = 6 months. Cut food and fun by $75 and save $225: 4 months.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Goal Budget

Pick something you want to buy. Make a real or pretend monthly budget that reaches the goal, then find one variable cost to cut to get there sooner.

3Simple interestI = P × r × t›
I can calculate simple interest. 8.5
Interest is the reward for saving or the cost of borrowing. The principal (P) is the starting amount. The rate (r) is the percent per year. Simple interest is earned only on the principal: I = P × r × t.

Example

$800 at 4% for 3 years: 800 × 0.04 × 3 = $96. Total: $896.

Try it

$
🧭
Expedition: Bank Shopper

Find the interest rate on 2 real savings accounts for youth. Calculate the simple interest on $500 for one year at each.

4Compound interestInterest on interest›
I can calculate compound interest and explain why time matters. 8.5
Compound interest is earned on the principal and on interest already earned. Each year, multiply by (1 + rate). The longer you leave it, the faster it grows. It works the same way against you when you owe money.

Example

$1,000 at 10% compound: Year 1 $1,100 → Year 2 $1,210 → Year 3 $1,331. Simple interest would give only $1,300.

5Saving vs. investingRisk and return›
I can compare saving and investing, including risk and return. 8.5
SavingInvesting
GoalKeep money safe for soonGrow money over years
ExamplesSavings account, GICStocks, mutual funds, ETFs
RiskLowHigher: value can go down
ReturnLow, steadyCan be higher, but not guaranteed
Return is the gain or loss, usually shown as a percent. Risk is the chance of losing value. Higher possible return usually means higher risk.

Paycheques, Taxes & Worker Rights Cheat Sheet · CEFL 8

Tax brackets: each portion of income is taxed at its own rate. Only dollars above a line pay the higher rate.

Progressive: higher incomes, higher rate · Flat: same rate for all · Regressive: lower incomes pay a bigger share.

Social contract: we pay taxes to fund shared services (health care, schools, roads, police).

Safety rights: right to know · right to participate · right to refuse unsafe work.

Compound interest: multiply by (1 + r) each year. $1,000 at 10% → $1,100 → $1,210 → $1,331.

Saving: safe, low return, for soon. Investing: riskier, possibly higher return, for later. Return % = gain ÷ start × 100.

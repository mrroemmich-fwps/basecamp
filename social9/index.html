Grade 9 Social Studies

Canadian government, rights and the economy, and how decisions affect your quality of life.

Unit 1

Citizenship & Collective Rights

What does it mean to be Canadian, and what rights come with it? In this unit you'll explore the Charter of Rights and Freedoms, the difference between individual and collective rights, Treaties 6, 7 and 8, and how immigration shapes who we are.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • describe the rights in the Charter and the responsibilities that go with them 9.1 · C
  • tell individual rights from collective rights 9.1 · C, I
  • explain the collective rights of Francophones 9.1 · I
  • explain how treaties and laws recognize First Nations, Métis and Inuit rights 9.1 · PADM
  • describe how immigration policy works and how it shapes Canada 9.1 · GC, I

2Lessons

1The CharterRights and responsibilities›
I can describe the rights in the Charter and the responsibilities that go with them. 9.1 · C
The Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms became part of the Constitution in 1982. It protects people from unfair treatment by governments. Rights come with responsibilities: free speech means not spreading hate; the right to vote means taking time to vote.
Charter rightsExample
Fundamental freedomsReligion, expression, peaceful assembly
Democratic rightsCitizens 18+ can vote
Mobility rightsLive and work in any province
Legal rightsA lawyer and a fair trial if arrested
Equality rightsNo discrimination based on race, religion, sex, age, disability…
Language rightsEnglish and French are official languages

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Rights in Real Life

Find a news story where someone's Charter right was involved. Explain which right and what happened.

2Individual vs. collective rightsMe and us›
I can tell individual rights from collective rights. 9.1 · C, I
Individual rights belong to every person (free speech, a fair trial). Collective rights belong to certain groups because of their history and place in Canada. They protect the group's language, culture and identity. In Canada, collective rights belong to Francophones, Anglophones in Quebec, and First Nations, Métis and Inuit peoples.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Rights Sort

Make a two-column chart of 6 individual and 4 collective rights, with a real example of each.

3Francophone rightsLanguage and education›
I can explain the collective rights of Francophones. 9.1 · I
French and English are Canada's official languages. Section 23 of the Charter gives qualifying parents in Francophone minority communities, like those in Alberta, the right to have their children educated in French where numbers warrant. Alberta has Francophone school authorities and a long Francophone history, from fur traders to towns like St. Paul, Bonnyville and Falher.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Francophone Alberta

Find one Francophone community, school or festival in Alberta. Share 3 facts about it and one French phrase you learned.

4Treaties and Indigenous rightsTreaties 6, 7 and 8›
I can explain how treaties and laws recognize First Nations, Métis and Inuit rights. 9.1 · PADM
Treaties are agreements between First Nations and the Crown. Almost all of Alberta is covered by Treaty 6 (1876), Treaty 7 (1877) and Treaty 8 (1899). First Nations understood treaties as sacred agreements to share the land. The Crown often saw them as giving up land. The Constitution Act, 1982 (section 35) recognizes and affirms Aboriginal and treaty rights. Alberta is home to the Métis Settlements, the only Métis land base in Canada. The Indian Act (1876) controlled many parts of First Nations life and is still in effect.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Treaty Territory

Find out which treaty area your school is in and which Nations signed it. Make a map with the date and one promise from the treaty.

5ImmigrationWho comes, and how?›
I can describe how immigration policy works and how it shapes Canada. 9.1 · GC, I
Canada's government decides how many immigrants to accept each year and in which categories: economic (workers with needed skills), family (joining relatives) and refugees (fleeing danger). Quebec chooses many of its own economic immigrants, partly to protect the French language. Decisions depend on the economy, labour shortages, population and humanitarian needs.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Journey Story

Interview someone who immigrated to Canada (or research a newcomer's story). What brought them here? What challenges did they face?

Unit 2 · On the trail now

Resources, Trade & the Economy

Why does gas cost what it does? Why do so many Alberta jobs depend on the United States? In this unit you'll learn how economies work, how Canada's differs from the U.S., and how decisions about resources, trade and workers affect your quality of life.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • explain needs, wants, scarcity and supply and demand 9.2
  • compare market and mixed economies, and explain why Canada is a mixed economy 9.2
  • describe the trading relationship between Canada and the U.S. 9.2
  • explain how Alberta's natural resources create jobs 9.2
  • explain how government decisions about resources and the environment affect quality of life 9.2
  • describe what labour unions do and how they affect workers 9.2
💼 CEFL connection: In CEFL 9 you're learning about getting hired and the job market. This unit explains why some industries grow and others shrink.

2Lessons

1What is an economy?Needs, wants, supply and demand›
I can explain needs, wants, scarcity and supply and demand. 9.2
An economy is how a society makes, shares and uses goods and services. There's never enough of everything for everyone. That's scarcity, so we have to make choices.

Supply and demand

  • Supply: how much of something is available.
  • Demand: how much people want it.
  • High demand + low supply → price goes up. Low demand + high supply → price goes down.

Example A new gaming console

When a new console comes out, everyone wants one (high demand) but stores have only a few (low supply). Prices from resellers shoot up. A year later, stores have lots and fewer people are rushing, so prices drop.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Price Tracker

Track the price of one thing (gas, eggs, a game) for 3 weeks. Graph it and explain any change using supply and demand.

2Market and mixed economiesWho decides?›
I can compare market and mixed economies, and explain why Canada is a mixed economy. 9.2
In a market economy, buyers and businesses make most decisions. In a mixed economy, businesses make many decisions but the government also steps in to provide services and set rules.
More market (like the U.S.)Mixed (like Canada)
Health careMany people pay through private insurance, often from workPublic health care, paid for by taxes
Government roleSmallerLarger: social programs, subsidies, regulations
TaxesOften lowerOften higher, to pay for programs

The U.S. is also mixed. It just leans more toward the market side than Canada does.

Example Breaking your arm

In Alberta, a hospital visit for a broken arm is covered by public health care. That's government stepping in, which is a sign of a mixed economy.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Debate Prep

Write a one-minute speech arguing for MORE government involvement in one area (like daycare or housing), then a one-minute speech arguing for LESS. Which was easier to argue, and why?

3Canada and the U.S.: trading partnersWhat we buy and sell›
I can describe the trading relationship between Canada and the U.S. 9.2
Exports are goods we sell to other countries. Imports are goods we buy from them. The United States is Canada's biggest trading partner by far, and most of Canada's exports go there.

Alberta and the U.S.

  • Alberta sells oil, natural gas, beef and grain to the U.S.
  • Alberta buys cars, machinery, electronics and fresh produce from the U.S.
  • Canada, the U.S. and Mexico have a trade agreement called CUSMA that sets rules for trade between them.

Example Why trade decisions matter to jobs

If the U.S. adds a tariff (a tax on imports) to Canadian goods, those goods cost more in the U.S., so Americans may buy less. Alberta companies sell less and may cut jobs.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Label Hunt

Check 10 products at home and record where each was made. How many came from the U.S.? Make a chart and bring it to class.

4Alberta's resources and jobsEnergy, farming and forests›
I can explain how Alberta's natural resources create jobs. 9.2
Natural resources are things from nature that people use: oil, gas, soil, water, trees. Many Alberta jobs depend on them, directly or indirectly.
ResourceJobs
Oil and gasHeavy equipment operator, welder, pipefitter, engineer, truck driver
FarmlandFarmer, rancher, agricultural mechanic, grain elevator operator
ForestsForestry technician, sawmill worker, wildland firefighter

Example Boom and bust

When oil prices are high, companies hire and towns grow (a boom). When prices drop, projects stop and workers are laid off (a bust). That's why many Albertans think about having transferable skills.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Resource Career Card

Use ALIS to research one Alberta career that depends on a natural resource. Make a card showing what they do, the training needed and the average pay.

5Governments, resources and the environmentBalancing jobs and nature›
I can explain how government decisions about resources and the environment affect quality of life. 9.2
Quality of life is how good life is for people: income, health, safety, clean air and water, free time. Governments try to balance jobs and money with protecting the environment.

Example A new mine

A company wants to open a mine near a river.
  • Supporters: hundreds of jobs, tax money for schools and hospitals.
  • Concerns: the river could be polluted, harming fish, farms, drinking water and nearby First Nations communities.
  • Governments may approve it with strict rules, change the plan, or say no. Every choice affects someone's quality of life.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Town Hall

Pick a real Alberta resource issue in the news. Write a short letter to your MLA explaining your position, using one fact for each side. Show your teacher before sending.

6Labour unionsWorkers bargaining together›
I can describe what labour unions do and how they affect workers. 9.2
A labour union is a group of workers who join together to negotiate with their employer about pay, safety and working conditions. This is called collective bargaining.

How unions work

  • Members pay union dues, usually taken right off their paycheque.
  • The union and employer agree on a contract (a collective agreement).
  • If they can't agree, workers may go on strike (stop working), or the employer may lock workers out.

Different points of view

Supporters say unions get fairer pay and safer workplaces. Critics say unions can raise costs for businesses and taxpayers, and strikes disrupt services. Many workers in Alberta, like nurses, teachers and grocery workers, belong to unions or professional associations.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Union Interview

Interview an adult who is in a union or professional association. Ask what it does for them and one thing they'd change. Share both sides with the class.

Unit 3

Laws, Courts & the Charter

Who makes Canada's laws, and what happens when someone breaks one? In this unit you'll learn the three branches of government, follow a bill as it becomes law, see how MPs and senators are chosen, explore the justice system and the Youth Criminal Justice Act, and find ways citizens can make their voices heard.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • describe the executive, legislative and judicial branches 9.1 · PADM
  • explain how a bill becomes a law 9.1 · PADM
  • explain how MPs and senators are chosen and the role of parties and media 9.1 · C, PADM
  • describe how citizens take part in the justice system, and the goals of the YCJA 9.1 · C
  • describe responsible ways citizens can influence government 9.1 · C

2Lessons

1Three branchesMake, carry out, interpret›
I can describe the executive, legislative and judicial branches. 9.1 · PADM
BranchWhoJob
ExecutiveThe Prime Minister and Cabinet (plus the King, represented by the Governor General)Proposes laws and runs the government day to day
LegislativeParliament: the House of Commons and the SenateDebates and passes laws
JudicialCourts and judges, up to the Supreme Court of Canada (9 judges)Interprets laws and decides if they follow the Charter

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Who's Who

Find out who your MP, MLA, mayor or reeve, and a Supreme Court justice are. Make a "who's who" chart for your family.

2How a bill becomes lawStep by step›
I can explain how a bill becomes a law. 9.1 · PADM
A bill is a proposed law. In the House of Commons: first reading (introduced) → second reading (debate) → committee study (details, changes) → report → third reading (final vote). Then the Senate does the same. Finally, the Governor General gives royal assent, and it becomes law.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Bill Tracker

Find a real bill being debated in Parliament or the Alberta Legislature. Track which stage it's at and what it would change.

3Elections, parties and mediaChoosing representatives›
I can explain how MPs and senators are chosen and the role of parties and media. 9.1 · C, PADM
MPs are elected by voters in each riding and answer to those voters. Senators are appointed by the Governor General on the Prime Minister's advice and serve until age 75. Political parties are groups with shared ideas who run candidates. The party with the most seats usually forms government, and its leader becomes Prime Minister. Media informs voters, questions leaders and can show bias.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Platform Compare

Pick 2 parties and one issue you care about. Compare what each party's website says about it.

4The justice systemJuries, advocates and the YCJA›
I can describe how citizens take part in the justice system, and the goals of the YCJA. 9.1 · C
Citizens take part by knowing the law, serving on a jury (adult citizens) and advocating for fair treatment. Groups like the John Howard Society and the Elizabeth Fry Society help people in the justice system rebuild their lives. The Youth Criminal Justice Act (YCJA) applies to youth aged 12 to 17. It focuses on accountability, rehabilitation and reintegration, and usually protects a young person's identity.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Court Visit

Visit a courthouse open session (with an adult), watch a mock trial video, or interview a lawyer or police officer about their job. Write 3 things that surprised you.

5Making your voice heardInfluencing government›
I can describe responsible ways citizens can influence government. 9.1 · C
Citizens influence government by voting, writing letters or emails to representatives, signing petitions, peaceful protest, lobbying (organized groups pushing for a cause) and joining a party. Even before 18, you can contact your MP or MLA.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Write Your Rep

Write a respectful email or letter to your MP or MLA about an issue you care about. Share any reply you get.

Unit 4

Consumers, Taxes & Quality of Life

What makes a good life, and can you buy it? In this unit you'll explore quality of life, consumerism and how advertising pushes us to buy, what taxes pay for, how social programs help Canadians, and how political parties disagree about all of it.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • identify indicators of quality of life 9.2 · ER
  • explain consumerism and how advertising and media affect what we buy 9.2 · ER
  • explain the purposes of taxation and the tax base 9.2 · PADM
  • describe social programs and compare government intervention in Canada and the U.S. 9.2 · ER, GC
  • compare how political parties' values show in their platforms 9.2 · PADM
💰 CEFL connection: CEFL 8 looks at tax brackets and what taxes pay for. This unit shows the "why" behind them.

2Lessons

1Quality of lifeWhat makes a good life?›
I can identify indicators of quality of life. 9.2 · ER
Standard of living is about money and things (income, housing, cars). Quality of life is bigger: health, safety, education, clean environment, free time, relationships and freedom. Governments measure it with indicators like life expectancy, literacy rate and income.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Good Life Survey

Ask 5 people of different ages what makes a good life. Sort their answers into standard of living and quality of life.

2Consumerism and adsBuy, buy, buy?›
I can explain consumerism and how advertising and media affect what we buy. 9.2 · ER
Consumerism is the idea that buying more goods is good for people and the economy. It creates jobs, but it can also create debt, waste and pollution. Advertising and influencers use data about you to target ads and make you feel you need things.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Buy Nothing Day

Go 3 days without buying anything you don't need. Track every time an ad tempted you, and how you felt.

3TaxesWhat they pay for›
I can explain the purposes of taxation and the tax base. 9.2 · PADM
Taxes pay for things we share: health care, schools, roads, police, firefighters and parks. The tax base is everything a government can tax: incomes, purchases, property and businesses. A bigger tax base means more money for services. The underground economy (cash jobs not reported) shrinks the tax base.
TaxWho collects it
Income taxFederal and provincial governments
GST (5%)Federal (Alberta has no provincial sales tax)
Property taxMunicipalities and schools

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Tax Trail

Pick one public service you used this week (a road, a hospital, a park). Find out which level of government pays for it.

4Social programsCanada and the U.S.›
I can describe social programs and compare government intervention in Canada and the U.S. 9.2 · ER, GC
Social programs are government services that support people's well-being: public health care, Employment Insurance, the Canada Pension Plan, the Canada Child Benefit and Old Age Security. Canada's mixed economy generally has more government programs than the U.S., where more services (like much of health care) are paid for privately or through employers.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Program Profile

Choose one social program. Find out who it helps, how it's paid for, and one argument for and against expanding it.

5Parties and platformsDifferent values, different plans›
I can compare how political parties' values show in their platforms. 9.2 · PADM
A platform is a party's plan if elected. Parties disagree about how much government should do. Some favour lower taxes and less government (more individual choice). Others favour more social programs, paid for with taxes (more shared support). Most Canadian parties fall somewhere in between.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Platform Detective

Choose one issue (housing, health care, jobs). Compare what 2 or 3 parties' platforms say about it, using their own websites. Stay neutral and just report.

Unit 5

Environment & Our Future

Alberta's land, water and resources pay for schools and hospitals, create jobs and give us places to hunt, fish and camp. But using them has costs. In this unit you'll explore how governments make resource and environment decisions, weigh different viewpoints, read data about trends, and build your own position on an issue that will shape your future.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • explain how government decisions about resources affect quality of life 9.2 · ER, LPP
  • assemble and assess different viewpoints on an environmental issue Skills · 9.2
  • read graphs and data to identify trends Skills
  • distinguish fact from opinion in media about the environment Skills · media literacy
  • develop and defend a position using evidence Skills · critical thinking

2Lessons

1Resources and decisionsWho decides, and who's affected?›
I can explain how government decisions about resources affect quality of life. 9.2 · ER, LPP
In Canada, provinces own and manage most natural resources (oil, gas, forests, minerals). Governments collect royalties when companies use them, which helps pay for services. Decisions involve trade-offs between jobs and revenue, the environment, and the rights of First Nations and Métis communities who must be consulted.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Resource Map

Map 3 resource industries near where you live. For each, list one benefit and one concern for your community.

2ViewpointsStakeholders at the table›
I can assemble and assess different viewpoints on an environmental issue. Skills · 9.2
A stakeholder is anyone affected by a decision. For a new mine or wind farm, stakeholders might include workers, the company, landowners, nearby First Nations, environmental groups, tourism businesses and future generations. Each has interests, and each viewpoint deserves a fair hearing.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Role Play Hearing

Hold a mock public hearing on an Alberta issue. Each person plays one stakeholder and argues their interests. Then vote as "council."

3Reading the dataTrends and graphs›
I can read graphs and data to identify trends. Skills
A trend is a general direction over time: rising, falling or steady. Read the title, axes and units first. Check the source, and watch for graphs that start the axis above zero to exaggerate a change.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Trend Spotter

Find one graph about Alberta's energy, water or wildlife from a government or StatCan site. Describe the trend and one possible cause.

4Fact, opinion and mediaSorting the noise›
I can distinguish fact from opinion in media about the environment. Skills · media literacy
Environmental issues get heated. Look for facts (data, measurements, sources) and opinions (loaded words, predictions, what someone "should" do). Check who funded the message: a company, a government, an advocacy group?

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Two Sides Media

Find two articles or videos with different views on the same environmental issue. Highlight facts and opinions in each.

5My positionEvidence-based and respectful›
I can develop and defend a position using evidence. Skills · critical thinking
Use the issue-analysis model: state the issue as a question, list the positions, give reasons for each, then choose your position and back it with evidence. A strong position also shows you understand the other side and suggests a compromise where possible.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Future Forum

Present your position on an Alberta environmental issue in a short speech, poster or video. Invite a community member or local councillor to hear it.

Citizenship & Collective Rights Cheat Sheet · Grade 9 Social Studies

Charter (1982): fundamental freedoms · democratic · mobility · legal · equality · language rights. Protects you from unfair government action.

Individual rights belong to everyone. Collective rights belong to groups: Francophones, Anglophones in Quebec, First Nations, Métis and Inuit.

Section 23: minority-language education (French schools in Alberta).

Treaties in Alberta: Treaty 6 (1876) · Treaty 7 (1877) · Treaty 8 (1899). Section 35 (Constitution Act, 1982) affirms Aboriginal and treaty rights. Alberta has the Métis Settlements.

Immigration classes: economic · family · refugee. Quebec selects many of its own.

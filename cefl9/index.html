Unit 1

Skills, Identity & the World of Work

Robots, apps and global competition are changing which jobs exist. In this unit you'll look at how automation and globalization reshape work, sort industries into growing, stable and declining, find reliable labour market data, and see how volunteering, entrepreneurship and language skills can open new paths.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • analyze how automation and globalization are reshaping jobs 9.1
  • identify growing, stable and declining industries using data 9.1
  • find labour market information from reliable sources 9.1
  • explain how volunteering and entrepreneurship build skills and career interest 9.3
  • identify careers where bilingualism is an asset 9.3
👥 Side by side: CEFL 8 is learning how the labour market changes. You're going deeper into where it's heading.

2Lessons

1AutomationMachines doing human tasks›
I can analyze how automation and globalization are reshaping jobs. 9.1
Automation uses technology (robots, software, AI) to do tasks people used to do. It can disrupt jobs, especially repetitive ones, but it also creates jobs (installing, fixing, programming and supervising machines). Globalization connects labour markets across borders, so you may compete with, or work with, people around the world.
Less likely to be automatedMore likely
Jobs needing hands-on skill in changing places (electricians, plumbers)Repetitive data entry
Caring and people skills (nurses, early childhood educators)Simple assembly-line tasks

2Where's the work going?Growing, stable, declining›
I can identify growing, stable and declining industries using data. 9.1
Industries can be growing (more jobs each year), stable or declining. Clues: labour market trends, government policy (new rules or funding), investment (companies building new plants) and technology. Career success depends on where the market is going, not just where it is.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Industry Sort

Use ALIS or Job Bank to sort 6 Alberta industries into growing, stable or declining. Back up each with one piece of data.

3Reliable LMIWhere to find the facts›
I can find labour market information from reliable sources. 9.1
Good labour market information comes from government databases (ALIS, Job Bank, Statistics Canada), industry reports and professional associations. Be careful with social media posts about "jobs that pay $100K with no training."

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Source Check

Find one career claim online and check it against ALIS or Job Bank. Was it accurate?

4Learning by doingVolunteering and entrepreneurship›
I can explain how volunteering and entrepreneurship build skills and career interest. 9.3
Volunteering lets you try different kinds of work and places. Entrepreneurship (starting your own business) teaches you how businesses run: a business plan, managing money, taking risks and sometimes managing people. Skills grow through formal experiences (courses, training) and informal ones (learning from family).

Quick check

5Language as a skillBilingual careers›
I can identify careers where bilingualism is an asset. 9.3
Some Canadian careers require English and French (many federal government jobs). In others, any second language is an asset: health care, tourism, customer service, law enforcement and trades serving newcomers. Languages can be learned formally (classes) and informally (family, community).

🧭
Expedition: Language Asset

Getting Hired

The job market is changing fast, and the people who get hired know how to show what they're worth. In this unit you'll decode job postings, build a personal brand, tailor your résumé, find where the jobs are, manage your digital identity and speak up for yourself in an interview.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • read a job posting and pick out the skills and qualifications it asks for 9.1
  • write a personal brand statement 9.1
  • tailor my résumé to a specific opportunity 9.1
  • use job search tools, networks and employment services 9.3
  • explain the risks and benefits of my digital identity 9.3
  • practise interviews and advocate for myself 9.1
✍️ Language Arts connection: In LA 9 you're learning how to write a résumé and cover letter. Here you'll learn how to aim them at the right job and stand out.

2Lessons

1Decoding a job postingWhat are they really asking for?›
I can read a job posting and pick out the skills and qualifications it asks for. 9.1
Job postings list qualifications (training or certificates you need) and skills (what you can do). Highlight both, then match them to your own.

Example A real-style posting

Front Counter Staff · Part-time
• Friendly and comfortable talking with customers
• Able to handle cash and make change
• Reliable: available weekends
• Food handler certificate an asset
• Must be 15 or older
  • Transferable skills: customer service, handling cash, reliability
  • Qualifications: age 15+, food handler certificate ("an asset" means nice to have, not required)

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Posting Match

Find 2 real job postings on Job Bank for jobs a 15–16-year-old could do. Highlight the skills and qualifications, then list which ones you already have.

2Your personal brandWho you are in one or two sentences›
I can write a personal brand statement. 9.1
Your personal brand is how you want others to see your skills, values and goals. A brand statement sums it up in 1–2 sentences.

Formula

I am a [quality] person who [skill or strength]. I want to [goal].

Examples

  • "I'm a hard-working, hands-on learner who loves fixing things. I want to become a heavy-duty mechanic."
  • "I'm a patient, caring person who's great with kids. I'm working toward a career in early childhood education."
Use your brand statement at the top of your résumé, in your cover letter and to answer "Tell me about yourself."

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Brand Check

Write your brand statement, then ask 3 people who know you if it sounds like you. Revise it using their feedback.

3Tailoring your résuméOne résumé doesn't fit every job›
I can tailor my résumé to a specific opportunity. 9.1
Your résumé shows your human capital: the education, skills and experience that make you valuable. Tailoring means moving the most relevant parts to the top and using the posting's own words.

Example Same person, two jobs

For a daycare helper jobFor a hardware store job
Lead with: babysitting, coaching kids' soccer, first aidLead with: building a shed with family, handling cash at the class store
Same person, but each résumé puts the best match first.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Two Versions

Make 2 versions of your résumé for 2 very different jobs. Highlight what you changed and why.

4Where are the jobs?Search tools, networks and services›
I can use job search tools, networks and employment services. 9.3
Many jobs are never posted online. Use every route: job websites, employer websites, job fairs, your network, and free employment services.

Routes to a job

  • Online: Job Bank, ALIS, company websites
  • In person: "Now hiring" signs, dropping off a résumé, job fairs
  • Your network: family, friends, coaches, teachers, neighbours
  • Employment services: free help with résumés and job searches, including services for youth, newcomers to Canada and Francophone job seekers

Example

Priya told her soccer coach she was looking for summer work. The coach's sister ran a garden centre and needed help. Priya got hired before the job was ever posted.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Job Map

Make a map or list of 10 places within walking or bus distance of home that hire teens. Note how each one hires: online, in person, or through people you know.

5Your digital identityEmployers look you up›
I can explain the risks and benefits of my digital identity. 9.3
Your digital identity is everything about you online: posts, photos, comments, usernames and what others post about you. Many employers search applicants' names before hiring.
RisksBenefits
Rude comments or bullying postsShowing off your art, sports or projects
Photos of unsafe or illegal behaviourVolunteering and community involvement
Sharing too much personal informationA professional profile (when you're older)

Quick check-up

  • Search your own name. What comes up?
  • Set personal accounts to private.
  • Ask: Would I be OK with my future boss seeing this?

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Digital Clean-Up

Search your name online with an adult, set your accounts to private, and remove anything you wouldn't want an employer to see. Write a short reflection on what you found.

6Interviews and self-advocacySpeak up for your value›
I can practise interviews and advocate for myself. 9.1
An interview is your chance to show your human capital. Self-advocacy means clearly explaining your skills, your value and what you need to do your best work.

Self-advocacy examples

  • Your value: "I'm the person my team counts on to show up on time, every time."
  • Your needs: "I learn best when I can see a task done once first. Could someone show me on my first shift?"
  • Your availability: "I can work weekends, but I have hockey Tuesday nights."
Being clear and honest about what you need is professional, not weak.

Before the interview

  • Practise "Tell me about yourself" using your brand statement.
  • Have one story ready for teamwork, solving a problem and a mistake you learned from.
  • Plan your route and arrive 10 minutes early.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Panel Practice

Do a mock interview with the Grade 8 panel, then interview a Grade 8 student about their goals so they can practise answering too.

Unit 3

Paycheques, Taxes & Worker Rights

Every spring, Canadians file a tax return. In this unit you'll read a T4 slip, learn how a tax return works, see how credits and deductions lower your tax, compare RRSPs and TFSAs, understand why all income (even tips and gig work) must be reported, and know your rights on the job.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • read a T4 slip and explain a tax return 9.4
  • calculate the effect of tax credits and deductions 9.4
  • compare RRSPs and TFSAs 9.4
  • explain how self-employment, tips and gig income are taxed and reported 9.4
  • describe my workplace rights and how benefits are delivered through taxes 9.4
👥 Side by side: CEFL 8 is learning tax brackets. Math 9 Unit 6 practises gross and net pay.

2Lessons

1The T4 and the tax returnYour year in one slip›
I can read a T4 slip and explain a tax return. 9.4
By the end of February, every employer sends you a T4 slip showing your total employment income and what was deducted (income tax, CPP, EI) for the year. You use it to file a tax return with the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA), usually by April 30. The return works out what you actually owe. If too much was deducted, you get a refund; if not enough, you pay the difference.
T4 boxWhat it shows
Box 14Employment income
Box 16CPP contributions
Box 18EI premiums
Box 22Income tax deducted

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Mock Return

Use a sample T4 and the CRA's free learning tools to walk through a simple tax return. Did the sample person get a refund or owe money?

2Credits and deductionsPaying less tax, legally›
I can calculate the effect of tax credits and deductions. 9.4
A deduction lowers your taxable income (like RRSP contributions or union dues). A tax credit lowers the tax you owe (like credits for tuition, charitable donations or disability). (Numbers in the practice are simplified.)

Example

You donate $200 to a food bank. With a made-up 15% credit, your tax owed drops by 200 × 0.15 = $30.

4All income countsSelf-employment, tips and gigs›
I can explain how self-employment, tips and gig income are taxed and reported. 9.4
All income is taxable and must be reported: tips, freelance work, delivery apps, online sales as a business and cash jobs. Self-employed people have no employer deducting tax, so they must set money aside and pay their own CPP (both the employee and employer portion). Not reporting income is illegal and shrinks the money for public services.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Gig Math

A delivery driver earns $500 in a week from an app. Research what costs and taxes they're responsible for, and estimate what they really keep.

5Rights and benefitsProtections and payments›
I can describe my workplace rights and how benefits are delivered through taxes. 9.4
Alberta's Employment Standards cover pay, overtime, breaks, vacation and termination. OHS gives the rights to know, participate and refuse unsafe work. Some government benefits, like the Canada Child Benefit and the GST/HST credit, are income-tested and paid through the tax system, which is another reason to file every year.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Rights Quick Guide

Make a one-page "know your rights" guide for a teen starting their first job in Alberta.

Unit 4

Credit & Debt

Credit can help you buy a car or go to school, or it can trap you for years. In this unit you'll learn how credit and credit scores work, why minimum payments are so expensive, how to spot predatory lenders, how to pay down debt with a plan, and how to start building good credit early.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • explain credit, debt and common types of consumer credit 9.5
  • calculate the cost of borrowing and compare minimum vs. faster payments 9.5
  • explain what affects a credit score 9.5
  • compare predatory lending with regular borrowing 9.5
  • apply a repayment strategy and calculate a debt-to-income ratio 9.5
🏔️ Avalanche HQ connection: The "avalanche" method of paying debt is real. You'll learn it in Lesson 5.

2Lessons

1Credit and debtBuy now, pay later›
I can explain credit, debt and common types of consumer credit. 9.5
Credit is the ability to borrow now and pay later. Debt is the money you owe, usually with interest. Common types: credit cards, lines of credit, personal and car loans, student loans and buy-now-pay-later plans.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Credit Offers

Collect 3 credit offers from ads, mail or websites (store cards, BNPL, loans). Find the interest rate and fees in the fine print.

2The minimum payment trapCost of borrowing›
I can calculate the cost of borrowing and compare minimum vs. faster payments. 9.5
Credit cards often charge around 20% a year, compounded. Paying only the minimum (often a small percent of the balance) can take years and cost hundreds or thousands in interest. Paying the full balance every month means you pay no interest on purchases.

Example

A $1,000 balance at 20%: interest is about 1,000 × 0.20 ÷ 12 ≈ $16.67 in the first month. Pay only $25 a month, and most of it goes to interest at first.

Try it

$
🧭
Expedition: Payoff Race

Use an online credit card payoff calculator. Compare paying the minimum vs. $100 a month on a $1,500 balance. Record the time and total interest.

3Credit scoresYour borrowing report card›
I can explain what affects a credit score. 9.5
In Canada, a credit score ranges from 300 to 900. A higher score means easier borrowing and lower interest rates. Factors: payment history (the biggest), credit utilization (how much of your limit you use; keep it low), length of history, types of credit and new applications.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Credit Plan

Write a 5-step plan to build good credit starting at 18 (for example: one card with a low limit, pay in full, on time, every month).

4Predatory lendingToo easy to be good›
I can compare predatory lending with regular borrowing. 9.5
Predatory lending targets people who are short on money. Payday loans and some high-interest instalment loans are fast and easy, but very expensive. In Alberta, a payday loan can cost up to $15 for every $100 borrowed for just a couple of weeks, which works out to a yearly rate in the hundreds of percent. Missing a payment can start a debt cycle.
Borrow $300 for 2 weeksCost
Payday loan ($15 per $100)$45
Credit card at 20% a yearAbout $2.30

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Ad Breakdown

Find a payday or quick-loan ad. List the tricks it uses and calculate the real cost of borrowing $500 from it.

5Getting out of debtAvalanche, snowball and DTI›
I can apply a repayment strategy and calculate a debt-to-income ratio. 9.5
Pay the minimum on everything, then put extra money toward one debt. Avalanche: highest interest rate first (saves the most money). Snowball: smallest balance first (quick wins keep you motivated). Your debt-to-income ratio (DTI) = monthly debt payments ÷ monthly gross income × 100. Lower is healthier.

Example

Debts: card A $400 at 22%, card B $1,200 at 19%, loan $3,000 at 8%. Avalanche: card A first (highest rate). Snowball: card A first too (smallest). Next, avalanche picks card B (19%), and so does snowball (next smallest). Sometimes both methods agree.

Try it

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Debt Plan

Make up a person with 3 debts. Plan their payoff with the avalanche and snowball methods. Which would you choose for them, and why?

Unit 5

Planning My Future

Is more school worth it? How will you pay for it? What if plan A doesn't work out? In this unit you'll figure out the true cost of a pathway, compare its return on investment, learn how student loans and financial aid work, explore earn-while-you-learn options, and build a post-secondary plan with a backup.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • calculate the true cost of a post-secondary pathway 9.2
  • compare the return on investment of different pathways 9.2
  • explain student loans and financial aid, including repayment 9.2
  • explain how work-integrated learning and other factors shape a pathway 9.2
  • build a post-secondary plan with a primary pathway and a backup 9.2

2Lessons

1The true costMore than tuition›
I can calculate the true cost of a post-secondary pathway. 9.2
The true cost = tuition + fees + books + living costs (rent, food, transportation) + opportunity cost: the money you could have earned working instead of being in school.

Example A made-up 2-year diploma

Tuition and fees $8,000/yr × 2 = $16,000. Books $1,500. Living at home, transportation $3,000. Opportunity cost: a $25,000/yr job × 2 = $50,000. True cost ≈ $70,500.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: True Cost Calc

Pick a real Alberta program. Find tuition, fees and books, estimate living costs, and add opportunity cost. Share the true cost.

2Is it worth it?Return on investment›
I can compare the return on investment of different pathways. 9.2
Return on investment (ROI) compares the total cost of a pathway with the expected income and opportunities over time. It depends on graduation rates, how many grads find jobs in that field, and the job market where you'll live. Personal values (enjoying your work, location, family) matter too.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: ROI Compare

Compare 2 pathways (for example, a trade and a university degree). Use ALIS wages to estimate how many years each takes to "pay back" its true cost.

3Student loans and aidBorrow wisely›
I can explain student loans and financial aid, including repayment. 9.2
Student loans are debt that must be repaid after you finish school, and some may charge interest, so check the current rules before you borrow. Grants, bursaries and scholarships don't need to be paid back. Borrow only what you need, and know your monthly payment before you sign.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Aid Explorer

Visit Alberta Student Aid's website. List the types of help available and one thing you'd need to qualify.

4Earn while you learnCo-op, internships and apprenticeships›
I can explain how work-integrated learning and other factors shape a pathway. 9.2
Work-integrated learning (co-op, internships, apprenticeships) lets you earn money and build experience during your training, lowering costs and improving job chances. Competitive programs may also look at portfolios, interviews, references and extracurriculars. If you move, your credentials may not be recognized everywhere. And it's never too late: adults change careers and return to school all the time.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Apprentice Q&A

Interview an apprentice, journeyperson or co-op student (or watch a Tradesecrets video). Write 3 things you learned about earning while learning.

5My post-secondary planPlan A, plan B, and why›
I can build a post-secondary plan with a primary pathway and a backup. 9.2
A strong plan has a primary pathway, a contingency (backup) and a rationale that covers money, personal fit and labour market data.

Example

Plan A: Registered Apprenticeship Program in Grade 11, then a heavy equipment technician apprenticeship. Plan B: parts technician certificate. Why: earn while learning, strong demand in Alberta, I love fixing engines. Money, fit and data all point the same way.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Plan A + B

Write your post-secondary plan with a primary pathway, a backup and a rationale. Present it to a family member or counsellor and add it to your portfolio.

Unit 1

Worldviews & Identity

Everyone sees the world through a lens shaped by family, culture, beliefs and experience. That lens is called a worldview. In this unit you'll explore your own worldview, learn the tools historians use, and get ready to see how different societies met, clashed and changed.

  • explain what a worldview is and describe parts of my own 8 overview · I
  • recognize that people have different perspectives Skills
  • tell primary sources from secondary sources and check for bias Skills
  • place events on a timeline and read historical maps Skills · TCC, LPP

2Lessons

1My worldviewThe lens I see through›
I can explain what a worldview is and describe parts of my own. 8 overview · I
A worldview is the set of beliefs, values and knowledge that shape how a person or society understands the world. It answers big questions: What matters most? How should people treat each other and the land? Where do we come from?

What shapes a worldview?

  • Family and culture
  • Religion or spirituality
  • Where and when you live (geography and time)
  • Language
  • Experiences and education

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Worldview Shield

Design a shield with 4 sections: family, beliefs, place and experiences. Fill each with words or pictures that shape your worldview.

2PerspectivesSame event, different views›
I can recognize that people have different perspectives. Skills
A perspective is a point of view on a specific event or issue. People with different worldviews often see the same event very differently. Historians try to hear all sides.

Example

When a new mine opens near a town: the mine owner sees jobs and profit; a local family sees paycheques but worries about water; a First Nation nearby may see harm to traditional hunting lands. One event, several valid perspectives.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Three Views

Pick a current issue in your community. Interview or research 3 people with different perspectives on it and summarize each.

3Thinking like a historianSources and bias›
I can tell primary sources from secondary sources and check for bias. Skills
A primary source was made at the time by someone who was there (a diary, photo, artifact, oral history). A secondary source was made later by someone studying it (a textbook, documentary). Every source has a creator with a perspective, so ask who made it and why.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Family Artifact

Bring in (or photograph) an old object or photo from your family. Explain what it tells us about the past and who made it.

4Timelines and mapsWhen and where›
I can place events on a timeline and read historical maps. Skills · TCC, LPP
A timeline shows events in order. CE (Common Era) years count up from year 1; BCE years count down to it. A century is 100 years: the 1500s are the 16th century. Historical maps show places, routes and borders at a certain time.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: My Timeline

Make a timeline of your life (or your family's history) with at least 8 events. Mark each decade.

5Worldviews and the landFirst Nations, Métis and Inuit perspectives›
I can describe how First Nations, Métis and Inuit worldviews connect to the land. 8.1
Many First Nations, Métis and Inuit worldviews see people as part of the natural world, not above it. Common teachings include respect, reciprocity (giving back), interconnection and responsibility to future generations. Each Nation has its own teachings, languages and ways of expressing them.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Land Connection

Find out whose traditional territory your school is on (Treaty 6, 7 or 8, or Métis homeland). Learn one teaching or place name from that Nation and share it.

Unit 2 · On the trail now

Japan: Isolation & Adaptation

For over 200 years, Japan closed its doors to most of the world. Then, in just a few decades, it changed faster than almost any country in history. In this unit you'll explore why Japan chose isolation, what happened when outsiders arrived, and how people keep traditions while the world changes around them.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • explain what a worldview is and what shapes it 8.1
  • describe how Japan's geography affected its worldview 8.1
  • explain how and why Japan isolated itself 8.1
  • describe what changed when the outside world arrived 8.1
  • explain how Japan adapted quickly while keeping traditions 8.1
  • describe how First Nations, Métis and Inuit worldviews have been influenced by other worldviews 8.1

2Lessons

1What is a worldview?The lens you see the world through›
I can explain what a worldview is and what shapes it. 8.1
A worldview is the way a person or society sees the world. It's built from beliefs (what you think is true), values (what you think matters) and knowledge (what you've learned).

What shapes a worldview?

  • Geography: where you live (mountains, ocean, prairie, city)
  • Time: when you live
  • Family, culture and religion
  • Contact with others: new people bring new ideas

Example Two students, two worldviews

A student who grew up on a farm near Lethbridge might value hard work, weather and land. A student who grew up downtown in Calgary might value transit, crowds and city jobs. Neither is wrong. Their surroundings shaped what they notice and care about.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: My Worldview Map

Make a poster or slide showing 3 beliefs, 3 values and 3 things you know that shape how you see the world, and where each one came from.

2Japan's geographyAn island nation›
I can describe how Japan's geography affected its worldview. 8.1
Japan is a chain of islands, with four main islands, surrounded by ocean. Most of the land is mountains, so people crowded onto small areas of flat land near the coast.

How geography shaped Japan

  • The sea was a natural wall that made invasion hard, and a source of food.
  • Mountains meant little farmland, so rice farming and fishing were very important.
  • Being separate helped Japan build its own unique culture, language and art.

Example Compare with Alberta

Alberta has no ocean and lots of open prairie. Japan is about half Alberta's size but has many times more people. Space, food and safety depended on the land and sea in very different ways.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Map Maker

Draw a labelled map of Japan showing the 4 main islands, Tokyo (once called Edo), Nagasaki and the Pacific Ocean. Add a key.

3Closing the doorsEdo Japan chooses isolation›
I can explain how and why Japan isolated itself. 8.1
From the early 1600s to 1868, Japan was ruled by a military leader called the shogun. In the 1630s the shogun closed Japan to almost all outsiders to keep control and protect Japanese ways.

Japan's social structure (top to bottom)

  1. Emperor: respected, but held little real power
  2. Shogun: the real ruler
  3. Daimyo: lords who controlled land
  4. Samurai: warriors who served the daimyo
  5. Peasants, artisans, merchants: farmers, craftspeople and traders

What isolation looked like

  • Most foreigners were not allowed in, and Japanese people could not leave.
  • Christianity was banned.
  • Only small amounts of trade were allowed, with Dutch and Chinese traders at one port, Nagasaki.
Result: about 200 years of peace and a strong Japanese culture, but Japan fell behind in technology.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Pros and Cons Debate

Prepare 3 reasons FOR and 3 reasons AGAINST Japan's isolation. Lead a 5-minute class debate.

4The black ships arriveContact in 1853›
I can describe what changed when the outside world arrived. 8.1
In 1853, American warships led by Commodore Matthew Perry sailed into Tokyo Bay. The Japanese called them the "black ships." Perry demanded that Japan open its ports to trade.

What happened next

  1. Japan's leaders saw the powerful steam-powered ships and cannons. Fighting back seemed impossible.
  2. Japan signed treaties opening ports to the United States, then to other countries.
  3. Many Japanese people felt the treaties were unfair, and anger at the shogun grew.
  4. In 1868 the shogun lost power, and the emperor was put back in charge. This was the start of the Meiji era.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Two Points of View

Write two short diary entries about the day the black ships arrived: one from a Japanese fisher and one from an American sailor.

5Rapid adaptationThe Meiji era›
I can explain how Japan adapted quickly while keeping traditions. 8.1
Japan's new leaders decided the best way to stay independent was to learn from the West, fast. They called it "rich country, strong army."
What changedWhat stayed
Railways, factories and telegraphs were builtThe Japanese language
Schools for all childrenShinto and Buddhist beliefs
A modern army and navy; the samurai class endedRespect for the emperor and family
Western-style clothes and governmentArts like tea ceremony, poetry and theatre

Opportunities and challenges

Opportunity: within about 40 years Japan became a world power. Challenge: the samurai lost their jobs and status, and many people struggled to keep old ways alive.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Then and Now

Interview an adult about the biggest change they've seen in their lifetime (phones, jobs, technology). What did people gain, and what did they lose? Compare it to the Meiji era.

6Change and tradition here at homeFirst Nations, Métis and Inuit worldviews›
I can describe how First Nations, Métis and Inuit worldviews have been influenced by other worldviews. 8.1
Like Japan, First Nations, Métis and Inuit peoples experienced contact with a very different worldview. Unlike Japan, many changes were forced on them, not chosen.

How contact influenced worldviews

  • Language: residential schools forced many children to stop speaking their languages.
  • Spirituality: some ceremonies were banned by Canadian law for many years.
  • Land: treaties and settlement changed how people could live on and use the land.

Example Keeping traditions today

Today, communities across Alberta are bringing back languages like Cree and Blackfoot through school programs, elders and apps, and ceremonies are practised openly. Like Japan in the Meiji era, people are finding ways to keep their traditions strong while the world changes.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Local Voices

With your teacher's help, find out which First Nations or Métis communities are near your school and one way they keep their language or traditions strong today. Share what you learned respectfully with the class.

Unit 3

The Renaissance

About 600 years ago, cities in Italy started a "rebirth" of art, science and ideas that changed Europe and, eventually, the world. In this unit you'll find out what sparked the Renaissance, how new ideas spread, how trade and exploration grew, and how it all shaped the western worldview many Canadians live with today.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • explain what the Renaissance was and why it started in Italy 8.2 · TCC, LPP
  • describe humanism and how it changed the way people saw the world 8.2 · I
  • explain how the printing press spread new ideas in science, religion and art 8.2 · GC
  • describe how geography, trade and competition led to exploration 8.2 · ER, LPP
  • connect Renaissance ideas to life in Canada today 8.2 · C, I

2Lessons

1A rebirthWhy Italy, why then?›
I can explain what the Renaissance was and why it started in Italy. 8.2 · TCC, LPP
Renaissance means "rebirth." From about the 1300s to the 1600s, Europeans rediscovered ancient Greek and Roman ideas. It began in Italian city-states like Florence and Venice, which were rich from trade across the Mediterranean. Wealthy families like the Medici paid artists and thinkers (they were patrons).

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Modern Patron

Who are today's "patrons" of art, music or sport (sponsors, grants, crowdfunding)? Find one example and explain how it works.

2HumanismPeople at the centre›
I can describe humanism and how it changed the way people saw the world. 8.2 · I
Humanism was a belief that individual people and their abilities matter, and that people can learn and improve through education. Art became more realistic (perspective, real human bodies). A "Renaissance person" is someone skilled in many areas, like Leonardo da Vinci: painter, inventor, engineer and scientist.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Renaissance Me

List 5 different skills you have or want to build (art, sport, tech, cooking, music). Make a "Renaissance résumé" for yourself.

3Ideas spreadPrinting press, science and religion›
I can explain how the printing press spread new ideas in science, religion and art. 8.2 · GC
Around 1450, Johannes Gutenberg built a printing press with movable type. Books became cheaper and faster to make, so more people learned to read and ideas spread quickly. Copernicus argued the Earth orbits the Sun; Galileo used a telescope to support it. Martin Luther's printed ideas split the Church (the Reformation).
ThenNow
Printing press spreads ideas across Europe in yearsThe internet spreads ideas around the world in seconds

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Then and Now Ideas

Compare the printing press to the internet or social media. Make a chart showing 3 ways they're similar and 3 ways they're different.

4Trade and explorationCompetition for routes and riches›
I can describe how geography, trade and competition led to exploration. 8.2 · ER, LPP
Europeans wanted spices, silk and gold from Asia, but land routes were long and controlled by others. Countries on the Atlantic, like Portugal and Spain, competed to find sea routes. New tools (the compass, better ships and maps) made it possible. In 1492, Columbus, sailing for Spain, reached the Americas.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Trade Route Map

Draw a map of one Renaissance trade or exploration route. Label what was traded and what dangers sailors faced.

5The western worldview todayRenaissance ideas in Canada›
I can connect Renaissance ideas to life in Canada today. 8.2 · C, I
Many ideas in Canada's dominant western worldview grew from the Renaissance: valuing the individual, science and evidence, public education, and trade and business. Seeing where these ideas came from helps us understand why other worldviews may see things differently.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Renaissance Today

Find 3 things in your life (a school subject, an invention, a value) with roots in the Renaissance. Make a poster connecting then and now.

Unit 4

The Aztecs & Spain

In 1519, two very different worldviews met in Mexico: the powerful Aztec Empire and Spanish explorers. In just two years, Tenochtitlan, one of the largest cities in the world, fell. In this unit you'll explore Aztec life, why Spain expanded, what happened at contact, and how it changed the Aztec people forever.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • describe the Aztec worldview and how it showed in daily life 8.3 · CC, I
  • explain what drove Spain to explore and expand 8.3 · ER, GC
  • explain how technology, allies and disease led to Spanish dominance 8.3 · TCC
  • describe how Aztec beliefs and identity changed after contact 8.3 · I, CC
  • compare contact in the Americas with contact in Japan 8.1, 8.3

2Lessons

1The Aztec worldTenochtitlan and daily life›
I can describe the Aztec worldview and how it showed in daily life. 8.3 · CC, I
The Aztecs (Mexica) built Tenochtitlan on an island in Lake Texcoco around 1325. It had causeways, canals, markets and floating gardens called chinampas. Their worldview centred on the gods, who they believed kept the sun moving and crops growing, and on duty to the community. Conquered peoples paid tribute (goods) to the empire.
Part of lifeHow the worldview showed
ReligionHuge temples, festivals and offerings to gods like Huitzilopochtli
LivelihoodFarming maize on chinampas, busy markets
GovernmentAn emperor, nobles and priests; tribute from conquered cities
FamilyEvery child went to school; duty to family and community

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: City Planner

Draw or build a model of Tenochtitlan with its causeways, temple and chinampas. Label how each part met people's needs.

2Spain expandsGold, God and glory›
I can explain what drove Spain to explore and expand. 8.3 · ER, GC
After 1492, Spain's worldview pushed it outward. Historians often sum up the reasons as "gold, God and glory": wealth from new lands, spreading Christianity, and fame and power for Spain and its explorers. Spain believed it had the right to claim lands for its king. This is called imperialism.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Motive Ranking

Rank gold, God and glory for Spain from most to least important. Defend your ranking in a paragraph or a short recording.

3Contact and conquest1519 to 1521›
I can explain how technology, allies and disease led to Spanish dominance. 8.3 · TCC
Hernán Cortés landed in Mexico in 1519 with a few hundred soldiers. By 1521, Tenochtitlan had fallen. How?
FactorWhat happened
DiseaseSmallpox, new to the Americas, killed huge numbers of people, including leaders
AlliesThousands of warriors from peoples like the Tlaxcalans, who were enemies of the Aztecs, joined the Spanish
TechnologySteel swords, armour, guns, cannons and horses
TranslationMalintzin (La Malinche), an Indigenous woman, translated and advised Cortés

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Cause Web

Make a cause-and-effect web showing why Tenochtitlan fell. Rank the causes and explain your top one.

4After contactA changed identity›
I can describe how Aztec beliefs and identity changed after contact. 8.3 · I, CC
Spain built Mexico City on top of Tenochtitlan. Temples were torn down and churches built. People were forced to work for Spanish landowners, and many converted to Christianity. Yet much survived: the Nahuatl language is still spoken by over a million people, and words like chocolate, tomato and avocado come from it. Today's Mexican identity blends Indigenous and Spanish cultures.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Word Detective

Find 5 words in English that come from Nahuatl or another Indigenous language of the Americas. Make a mini-dictionary.

Unit 5

Worldviews Today

Contact between worldviews didn't end centuries ago. It happens every day in Canada, online and around the world. In this unit you'll connect what you learned about Japan, the Renaissance and the Aztecs to today, analyze a current issue, and plan a way to make a difference.

  • plan and take action on an issue I care about Skills · social involvement

2Keeping traditions, adapting to changeHere at home›
I can describe how First Nations, Métis and Inuit communities keep traditions while adapting. 8.1 · I, CC
Contact with European worldviews brought huge pressure on First Nations, Métis and Inuit languages, spirituality and relationships with the land, including through residential schools. Today, many communities are revitalizing languages and traditions while using modern tools, like language apps, Indigenous-run businesses and media, and land-based learning.

3Media and other culturesStereotypes and stories›
I can explain how media shapes what we think about other cultures. Skills · media literacy
A stereotype is an oversimplified idea about a group. Movies, ads and posts can spread stereotypes, or break them. Ask: Who is telling this story? Is it someone from that culture? What's left out?

Quick check

🧭

Example

Positions: yes (recognition, reconciliation) · no (cost, tradition) · maybe (add a second sign).
Your position + your best reason.

Citizens can act by volunteering, raising awareness, writing letters, fundraising or organizing an event. A good action plan has a goal, steps, people, a timeline and a way to check if it worked.

Worldviews & Identity Cheat Sheet · Grade 8 Social Studies

Worldview: the beliefs, values and knowledge that shape how a person or society sees the world. Shaped by family, culture, spirituality, place, time, language and experience.

Perspective: a point of view on a specific event or issue.

Primary source: made at the time by someone there (diary, photo, artifact, oral history). Secondary: made later (textbook, documentary).

Centuries: the 1500s = 16th century. CE counts up, BCE counts down.

Key words: respect · reciprocity · interconnection · future generations.

Japan: Isolation & Adaptation Cheat Sheet · Grade 8 Social Studies

Worldview = beliefs + values + knowledge. Shaped by geography, time, family, culture, religion and contact with others.

Geography: 4 main islands, mostly mountains, surrounded by sea → natural protection, little farmland, a unique culture.

WhenWhat happened
1600s–1868Edo period: the shogun rules
1630sJapan closes its doors; small Dutch and Chinese trade only at Nagasaki
1853Commodore Perry's "black ships" arrive and demand trade
1868Meiji era begins: the emperor is restored and rapid change starts

Social structure: Emperor → Shogun → Daimyo → Samurai → Peasants, artisans, merchants

Meiji changes: railways, factories, schools, modern army, samurai class ended. Kept: language, Shinto and Buddhism, arts, respect for family.

Here at home: First Nations, Métis and Inuit worldviews were changed by contact, often by force (residential schools, banned ceremonies, land). Today languages and traditions are being revitalized.

The Renaissance Cheat Sheet · Grade 8 Social Studies

Renaissance: "rebirth," about 1300s–1600s, began in Italian city-states (Florence, Venice) rich from trade.

Patron: someone who pays artists and thinkers (the Medici family).

Humanism: individuals and education matter. Renaissance person = skilled in many areas (Leonardo da Vinci).

Aztecs & Spain Cheat Sheet · Grade 8 Social Studies

Tenochtitlan: Aztec capital built on Lake Texcoco around 1325. Chinampas = floating gardens. Tribute = goods paid by conquered peoples.

Spain's motives: gold (wealth) · God (spread Christianity) · glory (fame, power). Imperialism: taking control of other lands and peoples.

Contact: Cortés arrives 1519 · Tenochtitlan falls 1521.

Why Spain won: disease (smallpox) · Indigenous allies (Tlaxcalans) · technology (steel, guns, horses) · translation (Malintzin).

After: Mexico City built on the ruins · forced labour and conversion · Nahuatl survives · blended identity.

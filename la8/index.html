Grade 8 Language Arts

Reading, writing, viewing and speaking for school, home and the workplace.

Unit 1

Identity & Belonging

Who are you, and where do you belong? In this unit you'll read and listen to stories about identity, write about your own life, set goals for yourself as a reader and writer, and practise being a great member of a group.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • connect stories to my own life and experiences 1.1.1, 2.2.1
  • write a personal narrative paragraph 2.4.1, 2.4.3
  • explain how stories show culture and identity, including First Nations, Métis and Inuit stories 5.1.1, 5.1.2
  • set reading and writing goals for myself 1.1.4
  • work respectfully in a group 5.1.4, 5.2.1
👥 Side by side: Grade 9 is working on the same big question this unit. Look for chances to share stories across the room.

2Lessons

1What shapes who I am?Identity maps›
I can connect stories to my own life and experiences. 1.1.1, 2.2.1
Your identity is made of many parts: family, culture, language, interests, places, beliefs and experiences. When you read, look for connections: text-to-self ("That happened to me"), text-to-text ("That's like another story") and text-to-world ("That happens in real life").

Example An identity map

Put your name in the centre. Around it, add branches: family (oldest of 3), places (grew up on a farm near Ponoka), interests (hockey, drawing), language (English and Cree), values (loyalty). Each branch is a story you could tell.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Identity Map

Make your own identity map with at least 6 branches. Add a photo, drawing or object for each one.

2Telling my storyThe personal narrative paragraph›
I can write a personal narrative paragraph. 2.4.1, 2.4.3
A narrative paragraph tells one true moment from your life. It has a topic sentence that hooks the reader, details in order (what you saw, heard, felt), and a closing sentence that says why it mattered.

Example

The day I finally landed a kickflip, I learned that falling is part of getting better. For three weeks I practised in the Co-op parking lot after school. My shins were covered in bruises and my little brother laughed every time I wiped out. Then, on a cold Thursday, the board spun, my feet caught it, and I rolled away clean. Now, when something is hard, I remember those bruises and keep going.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Story Swap

Write a narrative paragraph about a moment that changed you. Read it to a family member and ask them to tell you a story from their life in return.

3Stories and cultureOral stories and belonging›
I can explain how stories show culture and identity, including First Nations, Métis and Inuit stories. 5.1.1, 5.1.2
Every culture passes on what it values through stories. Many First Nations, Métis and Inuit stories are oral: told and retold by Elders and Knowledge Keepers. They often teach lessons about respect, the land and how to live together. Some stories are only told at certain times or by certain people, so we treat them with respect.

How to respond to a story

  • Who is telling it, and to whom?
  • What does it teach about values (respect, sharing, courage)?
  • What does it show about the land or community?
  • How does it connect to your life?

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Family Story

Ask an older family member or community member to tell you a story they were told when they were young. Retell it to the class (with their permission) and explain what it teaches.

4Me as a learnerStrengths and goals›
I can set reading and writing goals for myself. 1.1.4
Good learners know their strengths and their stretch areas. A SMART goal is Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound.

Example

Not SMART: "Read more."
SMART: "I will read for 15 minutes, 4 nights a week, until the winter break, and log it in my planner."

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Goal Tracker

Set one SMART reading or writing goal. Track it for 4 weeks and write 3 sentences about how it went.

I can work respectfully in a group. 5.1.4, 5.2.1
Everyone does better when they feel they belong. In a group, use inclusive language, take turns, build on others' ideas, and give constructive feedback (helpful), not ridicule.

Unit 2 · On the trail now

Workplace Writing

Emails, letters, forms and instructions: the writing you'll actually use at a job. In this unit you'll learn when to sound formal, how to write a clear email, how to fill out forms without mistakes, and how to check your work before you hit send.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • choose formal or informal language to fit who I'm writing to 4.2.1, 4.1.4
  • write a clear workplace email 2.4.3
  • write a personal letter, like a thank-you letter 2.4.3
  • fill out work and home forms neatly and correctly 2.4.3, 2.1.2
  • follow and paraphrase written and spoken instructions 2.1.2, 4.3.4
  • proofread for capitals, punctuation and spelling 4.1.2, 4.2.2, 4.2.3
👥 Side by side: Grade 9 is writing résumés and cover letters right now. The formal-writing skills here are the first steps on that same trail.

2Lessons

1Formal or informal?Match your words to your reader›
I can choose formal or informal language to fit who I'm writing to. 4.2.1, 4.1.4
Informal language is for friends and family. Formal language is for bosses, teachers, customers and anyone you don't know well. Ask: Who is reading this?
Informal (to a friend)Formal (to a boss)
hey whats upHello Ms. Patel,
cant make it 2day 😬I'm sorry, but I won't be able to work my shift today.
idkI'm not sure. I will find out.
thx!!Thank you for your help.

Example Same message, two readers

To a friend: "Running late lol, see u at 4."
To a boss: "Hi Mr. Chen, I'm running about 15 minutes late because my bus was delayed. I'll be there by 4:15. Sorry for the trouble."
The formal version uses full words, no slang, a greeting and a reason.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Translator

Collect 5 texts or social posts (no names!) and rewrite each one in formal language, as if it were going to a boss.

2Writing a workplace emailFive parts every email needs›
I can write a clear workplace email. 2.4.3
A good work email has 5 parts: subject line, greeting, message, closing, name. Keep it short and get to the point.

Example Asking for a day off

Subject: Day off request for Friday, Dec. 4 Hello Ms. Patel, I would like to ask for Friday, December 4 off. I have a family event that day. I can work Saturday instead if that helps. Thank you, Jordan Lee
  1. Subject: says what it's about, with the date.
  2. Greeting: "Hello" + the person's name.
  3. Message: what you want, why, and an offer to help.
  4. Closing: "Thank you," or "Sincerely,"
  5. Name: your full name.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Inbox Helper

Write a real, polite email to a teacher, coach or club leader asking a question you actually have. Show it to your teacher before you send it.

3Personal lettersThank-you notes and more›
I can write a personal letter, like a thank-you letter. 2.4.3
A personal letter has a date, greeting, body, closing and signature. A good thank-you letter says what you're thankful for and why it mattered.

Example A thank-you letter

November 20, 2026 Dear Mr. Okafor, Thank you for letting our class tour your auto shop. I learned how an oil change works and why safety glasses matter. Seeing the tools up close made me think about becoming a mechanic. Sincerely, Riley

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Gratitude Letter

Write a real thank-you letter to someone who helped you or the class this year (a bus driver, custodian, coach or family member) and deliver it.

4Filling out formsJob applications and other forms›
I can fill out work and home forms neatly and correctly. 2.4.3, 2.1.2
Forms are often the first thing an employer sees. Read the whole form first, print neatly, and don't leave blanks.

Form tips

  • Read every instruction first. Some say "print in black ink" or "use CAPITAL LETTERS."
  • Write N/A (not applicable) if a question doesn't apply to you. Don't leave it blank.
  • Dates are often written YYYY-MM-DD (2026-11-20).
  • Keep private information private. Only put your SIN (Social Insurance Number) on a form from your actual employer, never on a practice form or website you don't trust.
  • Check spelling, then sign and date at the end.

Example Part of a job application

Position applying forGrocery clerk
Available to start2026-12-01
Previous work experienceVolunteer, school breakfast program, 2025–2026
Driver's licenceN/A

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Form Pro

Fill out a practice job application from your teacher with zero mistakes. Use made-up information for anything private.

5Following instructionsRead, listen, then say it back›
I can follow and paraphrase written and spoken instructions. 2.1.2, 4.3.4
To paraphrase is to say something again in your own words. Saying instructions back is the best way to check you understood them.

Example At a café job

Your supervisor says: "Before you leave, wipe the counters, restock the cups and lids, and lock the back door. Then text me."
  1. Paraphrase: "So: counters, cups and lids, lock the back door, then text you. Got it."
  2. Make a quick numbered list if there are more than 3 steps.
  3. If something isn't clear, ask before you start.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Recipe Run

Follow a written recipe at home from start to finish with no help, then teach a family member how to make it using only your own words.

6Proofread before you sendCapitals, punctuation and spelling›
I can proofread for capitals, punctuation and spelling. 4.1.2, 4.2.2, 4.2.3
One small mistake can make a good email look careless. Read it slowly, out loud, once for each thing on the checklist.

Proofreading checklist

  • Capitals: first word of each sentence, names, days and months, the word "I."
  • Punctuation: a period, question mark or exclamation mark at the end of each sentence.
  • Spelling: watch for there/their/they're, your/you're, to/too/two.
  • Make sense: read it out loud. Does it sound right?

Example Fix it

✗ "i can work monday if your ok with that"
✓ "I can work Monday if you're OK with that."

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Editor on Duty

Be the class editor for a week: proofread 3 classmates' emails or letters and help them fix every mistake before they hand them in.

Unit 3

Stories & Scripts

Every good story has a hook, a problem and characters you care about. In this unit you'll map the plot of short stories, study how characters change, find the tricks authors use to build suspense, and turn a story into a script you perform.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • map the parts of a plot and identify the main conflict 2.2.2
  • describe how a character changes and why 2.2.2
  • find hooks, foreshadowing, flashbacks and suspense 2.3.2
  • explain how setting and word choice create mood 2.2.3, 2.3.3
  • write and perform a short script 2.4.3, 4.3.1
👥 Side by side: Grade 9 is comparing a novel with its film. Your plot and character skills are exactly what they're using.

2Lessons

1The plot mountainMapping a story›
I can map the parts of a plot and identify the main conflict. 2.2.2
The introduction sets up characters and setting. The initial incident starts the problem. Rising action builds tension. The climax is the turning point. Falling action wraps things up, and the resolution ends it. The conflict is the main problem: person vs. person, vs. self, vs. nature or vs. society.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Movie Mountain

Pick your favourite movie or show episode and draw its plot mountain with all six parts labelled.

2Characters who changeTraits, motives and choices›
I can describe how a character changes and why. 2.2.2
Traits are what a character is like (brave, selfish). Motivation is why they act. A dynamic character changes by the end; a static one stays the same. To prove a trait, find what the character says, does and thinks, and how others react to them.

Example

At the start, Jordan won't pass the puck to the new kid. After the new kid covers for Jordan's mistake, Jordan passes to him for the winning goal. Jordan changes from selfish to a team player, motivated by respect.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Then and Now

Choose a character from a book, show or game who changes. Make a two-column chart: what they were like at the start and at the end, with proof for each.

3Hooks and suspenseThe author's toolbox›
I can find hooks, foreshadowing, flashbacks and suspense. 2.3.2
TechniqueWhat it doesExample
HookGrabs the reader right away"The phone rang at 3 a.m., and nobody ever calls at 3 a.m."
ForeshadowingHints at what's coming"I should have checked the ice."
FlashbackJumps back to an earlier time"Two years earlier, Dad had warned me…"
SuspenseKeeps you worried or curiousShort sentences, a ticking clock, a cliffhanger
Surprise endingA twist you didn't expectThe "monster" was the family dog

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Hook Challenge

Write 3 different opening sentences for the same story: one with a question, one with action, one with a strange detail. Ask 3 people which one makes them want to keep reading.

4Setting and moodHow a story feels›
I can explain how setting and word choice create mood. 2.2.3, 2.3.3
Setting is when and where. Mood is the feeling the reader gets (creepy, cozy, tense). Authors build mood with setting details, word choice and figurative language like similes ("cold as steel") and metaphors ("the hallway was a tunnel").

Same place, two moods

Cozy: "Snow drifted past the window while the woodstove crackled and the kitchen smelled like bannock."
Creepy: "Snow hissed against the glass. The stove had gone cold, and something scraped across the porch." Same setting, different words, different mood.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Mood Swap

Take a photo of one place at home or school. Write two short descriptions of it: one cozy, one creepy.

5From story to scriptWrite it, then perform it›
I can write and perform a short script. 2.4.3, 4.3.1
A script tells a story through dialogue (what characters say) and stage directions (in brackets, in italics: what they do and how they say it). When you perform, use your voice (volume, tone, speed) and body (gestures, facial expressions).

Example

AVA: (whispering) Did you hear that?
LIAM: (rolls his eyes) It's just the furnace.
AVA: (grabbing his arm) Furnaces don't knock.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Readers Theatre

With 2–4 classmates, turn a short story or fable into a 2-minute script. Rehearse and perform it for another class or record it.

Unit 4

Media Literacy

Ads, news, videos, websites and posts are all built by someone, for a reason. In this unit you'll read everyday media with a purpose, tell fact from opinion, check whether a source can be trusted, and spot the tricks advertisers use.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • read everyday texts like schedules, labels and websites for a purpose 2.1.3
  • tell fact from opinion 3.2.2
  • check whether a source is credible and current 3.2.3, 3.3.3
  • compare how TV, radio, print and online media tell the news 2.3.1
  • identify techniques ads use to persuade 2.3.3, 2.2.2
👥 Side by side: Grade 9 is looking at bias in the media. You're building the first tools for that same job.

2Lessons

1Reading everyday textsSchedules, labels and websites›
I can read everyday texts like schedules, labels and websites for a purpose. 2.1.3
Everyday texts are built to be scanned, not read top to bottom. Know your purpose first ("What time is the bus?"), then use headings, bold words, tables, icons and menus to jump to the answer.

Example A bus schedule

StopRun 1Run 2Run 3
Main St7:107:408:10
Library7:187:488:18
School7:308:008:30
You need to be at school by 8:15. Find "School," look for the latest time before 8:15: 8:00. Go up that column: catch the 7:40 bus at Main St.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Scavenger Scan

Find 5 everyday texts at home (a recipe, a label, a flyer, a schedule, a menu). Write one question for each and time how fast a family member can find the answer.

2Fact or opinion?Can it be proven?›
I can tell fact from opinion. 3.2.2
A fact can be proven true or false. An opinion is what someone believes or feels. Opinion clue words: best, worst, should, amazing, I think, everyone knows.

Examples

Fact: The Oilers play their home games in Edmonton.
Opinion: The Oilers have the best fans in the league.
Tricky: "Most people agree this is the best phone." Still an opinion, just dressed up.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Fact Hunt

Find a product review or a sports article. Highlight facts in one colour and opinions in another. Which was there more of?

3Can I trust it?Checking sources›
I can check whether a source is credible and current. 3.2.3, 3.3.3
Before you believe or share something, check it. Ask: Who made it? When? Why (to inform, sell, entertain, persuade)? Can I find the same information from another trusted source?

Example

A video says a new pill "melts belly fat overnight." Who made it? The company selling the pill. Why? To sell. Do doctors or health sites agree? No. Not credible.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Fact Checker

Find one surprising claim online. Check it with 2 trusted sources and report whether it's true, false or misleading.

I can compare how TV, radio, print and online media tell the news. 2.3.1

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: News Compare

Follow one local news story on two different media (a radio station and a website, for example). Write what each one included and left out.

I can identify techniques ads use to persuade. 2.3.3, 2.2.2

Unit 5

Persuasion & PSAs

Want a later bedtime, a new school club or people to stop littering? You'll need to persuade. In this unit you'll pick an audience and purpose, write a persuasive paragraph with strong reasons, design a public service announcement (PSA), and present it with confidence.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • identify the audience and purpose of a message 1.2.3, 3.1.1
  • write a persuasive paragraph with a clear position and reasons 2.4.3
  • revise and edit with feedback 4.1.1, 4.1.2
  • design a PSA using words and visuals 2.4.2, 4.3.2
  • present to an audience with voice and body language 4.3.1, 4.3.3

2Lessons

1Audience and purposeWho am I talking to, and why?›
I can identify the audience and purpose of a message. 1.2.3, 3.1.1
The audience is who will read or hear it. The purpose is what you want to happen. The same request sounds very different to a friend, a parent or a principal.

Example Asking for a skate park

To friends: "Let's get a skate park! Sign the petition."
To town council: "A skate park would give 200 local youth a safe place to be active after school."
Same goal, different words for each audience.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Change Maker

Pick a real change you want at home, school or in your town. Write who the audience is and what you want them to do.

2The persuasive paragraphPosition, reasons, call to action›
I can write a persuasive paragraph with a clear position and reasons. 2.4.3
Position (what you believe) → 3 reasons, each with an example or fact → counter-argument (what the other side might say, and your answer) → call to action (what you want the reader to do).

Example

Our school should have a recycling program in every classroom. First, it would keep hundreds of bottles out of the landfill each month. Second, the bottle deposits could raise money for field trips. Third, it teaches responsibility. Some people say it would be too much work, but student volunteers could collect bins once a week. Let's start a recycling team this month.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Letter of Change

Write a persuasive paragraph or letter about your Change Maker topic and send it to the real person who can make it happen (with your teacher's help).

3Revise and editMake it stronger, make it right›
I can revise and edit with feedback. 4.1.1, 4.1.2
Revising makes your ideas stronger (add a reason, cut what's off-topic, pick stronger words). Editing fixes mistakes (capitals, punctuation, spelling, complete sentences). Do revising first, then editing.

CUPS checklist

  • Capitals: sentences, names, places
  • Usage: words make sense, verbs match
  • Punctuation: periods, commas, question marks
  • Spelling: check tricky words

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Editing Buddy

Trade persuasive paragraphs with a classmate. Give 2 compliments, 1 revising idea and check their CUPS.

4Designing a PSAWords + visuals›
I can design a PSA using words and visuals. 2.4.2, 4.3.2
A public service announcement gives the public an important message, like staying safe on ice or not texting and driving. A strong PSA has a short slogan, one clear image, a fact and a call to action. Keep text big and simple.

Example A poster

Image: a phone on a dashboard. Slogan: "That text can wait." Fact: distracted driving is a leading cause of crashes. Action: "Put your phone in the back seat."

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: PSA Poster

Design a PSA poster or 30-second video for a real issue at your school or in your community. Ask to display or share it.

5Presenting with confidenceVoice and body language›
I can present to an audience with voice and body language. 4.3.1, 4.3.3
Your voice: loud enough for the back row, not too fast, with pauses. Your body: stand tall, face the audience, look up from your notes, use your hands to point to visuals. Practise out loud at least 3 times.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Pitch It

Present your PSA to a younger class, a family member or the school announcements. Ask for one piece of feedback.

Unit 6

My Career Portfolio

A portfolio shows who you are and what you can do. In this unit you'll research a career using real Alberta tools, find out what reading and writing it takes, practise the habits employers look for, and put together a portfolio you can keep building through high school.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • plan and carry out research on a career 3.1.1, 3.2.1
  • use ALIS to find the language skills a career needs 1.1.4
  • take point-form notes and cite my sources 3.3.2
  • describe and show the behaviours of a successful worker 5.2.1
  • build a portfolio and a language learning plan 1.1.4, 3.4.1
👥 Side by side: Grade 9 is building a transition plan for high school. Your portfolio is where that plan will start next year.

2Lessons

1Planning research5W + H questions›
I can plan and carry out research on a career. 3.1.1, 3.2.1
Good research starts with good questions. Use 5W + H: Who does this job? What do they do? Where do they work? When (hours, shifts)? Why is it needed? How do you get into it?

Example Career: electrician

What do electricians do? Where do they work? How long is the apprenticeship? What reading and math do they use on the job? How much do they earn in Alberta?

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Career Pick

Choose 3 careers you're curious about. Write 5W + H questions for the one you'll research first.

2Using ALISAlberta's career tool›
I can use ALIS to find the language skills a career needs. 1.1.4
ALIS (alis.alberta.ca) has an OCCinfo profile for hundreds of careers: duties, working conditions, education, wages and outlook. Many profiles also list the reading, writing and communication skills the job needs.

What to look for in a profile

  • Duties: what a typical day looks like
  • Working conditions: indoors, shifts, physical work
  • Educational requirements
  • Wage and salary in Alberta
  • Employment outlook: are workers needed?

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: ALIS Explorer

Find your career on ALIS. Record 3 duties, the education needed, the wage range and one reading or writing task the job uses.

3Notes and sourcesPoint form, in your own words›
I can take point-form notes and cite my sources. 3.3.2
Point-form notes use key words, not full sentences, and are in your own words. Always record where you found it (website name, title, date) so others can check it, and so you're not copying someone else's work.

Example

Source text: "Electricians typically complete a four-year apprenticeship program that combines on-the-job training with technical training."
Notes: • 4-yr apprenticeship • work + classes (ALIS, Electrician)

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Interview Notes

Interview someone about their job (5 questions). Take point-form notes and turn them into a short profile.

4Successful worker habitsWhat employers notice›
I can describe and show the behaviours of a successful worker. 5.2.1
Employers say these habits matter as much as skills: arrive on time, follow directions, ask for help when you need it, stay organized, prioritize tasks and take constructive feedback well.

Quick check

🏔️ Avalanche HQ connection: These are the same habits that earn AvCash in class. You're already practising for the job site.
🧭

I can build a portfolio and a language learning plan. 1.1.4, 3.4.1
A career portfolio is a folder (paper or digital) that shows your best work, skills and goals. Add to it every year.

What goes in

  • A cover page with your name and a career goal
  • Your career research
  • 2–3 pieces of your best writing
  • Certificates, awards, volunteer hours
  • A language learning plan: one reading, writing or speaking skill you need for your goal, and how you'll build it

Identity & Belonging Cheat Sheet · Grade 8 Language Arts

Connections: text-to-self · text-to-text · text-to-world.

Narrative paragraph: hook topic sentence → details in order (see, hear, feel) → closing sentence that says why it mattered.

Responding to a story: Who tells it? What values does it teach? What does it show about land or community? How does it connect to me?

SMART goal: Specific · Measurable · Achievable · Relevant · Time-bound.

Constructive feedback: say what works, then one specific idea to make it better.

Workplace Writing Cheat Sheet · Grade 8 Language Arts

Formal or informal? Ask: who is reading this? Friends and family = informal. Bosses, teachers, customers = formal (full words, no slang, polite).

Email: 1) Subject line 2) Greeting ("Hello Ms. Patel,") 3) Message: what, why, offer to help 4) Closing ("Thank you,") 5) Your name

Personal letter: date · greeting ("Dear…,") · body · closing ("Sincerely,") · signature. Thank-you letters say what and why.

Forms: read the whole form first · print neatly · N/A if it doesn't apply · dates as YYYY-MM-DD · keep private info private · sign and date.

Instructions: listen → say it back in your own words → make a list → ask if unsure.

Proofread for…Watch out for
CapitalsSentences, names, days, months, "I"
PunctuationEnd every sentence with . ? or !
Spellingthere/their/they're · your/you're · to/too/two
SenseRead it out loud

Stories & Scripts Cheat Sheet · Grade 8 Language Arts

Plot: introduction → initial incident → rising action → climax → falling action → resolution.

Conflict: person vs. person · vs. self · vs. nature · vs. society.

Character: traits (what they're like) · motivation (why) · dynamic (changes) or static (doesn't). Prove it with what they say, do and think.

Techniques: hook · foreshadowing (hints) · flashback (goes back) · suspense · surprise ending.

Mood: the feeling a story gives. Built with setting, word choice, similes (like/as) and metaphors.

Script: NAME: (stage direction) dialogue.

Media Literacy Cheat Sheet · Grade 8 Language Arts

Everyday texts: know your purpose, then scan headings, bold words, tables and icons.

Fact = can be proven. Opinion = a belief or feeling. Clue words: best, worst, should, amazing, I think.

Check a source: Who made it? When? Why? Can I confirm it somewhere trusted?

Media: TV (video, emotion) · radio (listen anywhere) · print (detail) · online (fast, but false info spreads).

Ad techniques: bandwagon · celebrity · expert · urgency · emotion · glittering words.

Persuasion & PSAs Cheat Sheet · Grade 8 Language Arts

Audience: who it's for. Purpose: what you want to happen.

Persuasive paragraph: position → 3 reasons with examples → counter-argument and answer → call to action.

Revise (better ideas) first, then edit with CUPS: Capitals · Usage · Punctuation · Spelling.

PSA: short slogan · one strong image · one fact · call to action · big, simple text.

Presenting: loud and slow · pause · stand tall · eye contact · practise 3 times.

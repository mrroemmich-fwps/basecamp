Grade 9 Language Arts

Stories, essays, media and interviews: communicating with confidence for high school and the job site.

Unit 1

Identity & Belonging

What makes you, you? And what does it take to belong somewhere new, like high school or a first job? In this unit you'll analyze characters' choices, find the theme of a story, read stories from different cultures, and write a short personal essay about your own identity.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • analyze how characters' choices and motives reveal who they are 2.2.2
  • identify the theme and point of view of a story 2.2.2
  • analyze how stories reflect the beliefs and traditions of a culture 5.1.1, 5.1.2
  • plan and write a three-paragraph personal essay 2.4.1, 2.4.3
  • assess my own role and effectiveness in a group 5.2.2, 5.2.3
👥 Side by side: Grade 8 is exploring the same big question. Share your favourite identity stories with them.

2Lessons

1Choices reveal characterMotives and plausibility›
I can analyze how characters' choices and motives reveal who they are. 2.2.2
Characters show who they are through their choices, especially hard ones. Ask: What did they choose? Why (motive)? Is their behaviour consistent? If they change, is the change believable (plausible), or does it come out of nowhere?

Example

Kai has always kept to himself. When a classmate is bullied, he finally speaks up, but only after the story shows him remembering how lonely he felt at his old school. The change is plausible because the author gave him a reason.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Choice Map

Pick a character from a book, show or game. Map 3 big choices they made, the motive behind each, and what each one shows about them.

2Theme and point of viewWhat's the story really about?›
I can identify the theme and point of view of a story. 2.2.2
The topic is one word (friendship). The theme is the lesson or message about it, written as a sentence ("True friends tell you hard truths"). Point of view is who tells the story: first person ("I"), third person limited (one character's thoughts) or third person omniscient (everyone's thoughts).

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Theme Song

Choose a song or short film (school-appropriate). Write its topic, its theme in one sentence and 2 pieces of evidence.

3Stories across culturesTraditions, beliefs and identity›
I can analyze how stories reflect the beliefs and traditions of a culture. 5.1.1, 5.1.2
Stories carry a culture's values, traditions and history. First Nations, Métis and Inuit stories, newcomer stories and family stories all teach about belonging. When analyzing, ask: What does this story value? What tradition or belief shows up? How does the time and place shape it?

Questions for any story

  • Whose voice is telling it?
  • What does the community in the story value most?
  • What traditions or beliefs appear?
  • How would the story change if someone else told it?

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Story Keeper

Record or write down a story from an Elder, grandparent or community member (with permission). Explain what it shows about their values and traditions.

4The personal essayThree paragraphs about you›
I can plan and write a three-paragraph personal essay. 2.4.1, 2.4.3
An essay has an introduction (hook + thesis: your main idea), body (examples that support it) and a conclusion (restate the idea, what it means for your future). Plan with a web or outline before writing.

Example outline

Thesis: Working on my uncle's ranch taught me who I am.
Body: calving season at 3 a.m. → responsibility; fixing fence with my uncle → patience and pride.
Conclusion: Those skills will follow me to high school and whatever job comes next.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Identity Essay

Write a three-paragraph essay about an experience that shaped who you are. Add it to your portfolio.

5My role in a groupHow effective am I?›
I can assess my own role and effectiveness in a group. 5.2.2, 5.2.3
Groups work best when people take roles: leader, recorder, timekeeper, motivator, presenter. Afterwards, assess yourself honestly: Did I do my part? Did I listen? Did I help resolve disagreements?

Quick check

🏔️ Avalanche HQ connection: Leading a group well is worth AvCash. Try leading a Grade 8 and 9 team on an Expedition.
🧭
Expedition: Lead the Way

Volunteer to lead a group task (in class, sports, a club or at home). Afterwards, rate yourself out of 5 on listening, organizing and encouraging, with a reason for each.

Unit 2 · On the trail now

Résumés, Cover Letters & Interviews

Getting a job starts with writing. In this unit you'll build a résumé, write a cover letter and a memo, complete applications, and practise the formal language that makes a great first impression in an interview.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • describe my skills and connect them to jobs I want 1.1.4
  • write a clear one-page résumé 2.4.3
  • write a cover letter for a specific job 2.4.3
  • write a workplace memo 2.4.3
  • complete a job application accurately 2.4.3, 2.1.3
  • use formal language and body language in an interview 4.2.1, 4.3.1
👥 Side by side: Grade 8s will read the same job postings and sit on the panel for your mock interviews. In CEFL 9 you're also building a personal brand statement. Use it in your cover letter!

2Lessons

1Know your skillsWhat do you bring to a job?›
I can describe my skills and connect them to jobs I want. 1.1.4
You already have skills employers want, even without a job yet. Transferable skills come from school, sports, chores, volunteering and hobbies.
What you didSkill an employer sees
Babysat younger siblingsResponsible, patient, child care
Played on a hockey teamTeamwork, showing up on time
Helped run the class storeHandling money, customer service
Fixed bikes with a friendProblem solving, mechanical skills

Example Turning an activity into a skill

"I play video games" → "I solve problems under pressure and work with a team online to reach a goal."

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Skills Inventory

Interview 2 adults who know you (family, coach, teacher). Ask them for 3 skills they see in you. Add the best ones to your career portfolio.

2Building a résuméOne page, easy to scan›
I can write a clear one-page résumé. 2.4.3
A résumé is a one-page summary of your skills and experience. Employers often look at it for less than a minute, so make it neat, short and easy to scan.

Example A first résumé

ALEX MARTIN
alex.martin.jobs@email.com · 780-555-0123

SKILLS

• Customer service and handling money · • Reliable and on time · • Basic first aid

EXPERIENCE

Volunteer, School Breakfast Program · Sept 2025–present
• Prepare and serve breakfast to 40 students each morning
• Keep the kitchen clean and follow food safety rules

EDUCATION

Grade 9 · Expected to start high school Sept 2027

REFERENCES

Available on request

The name, email and phone number above are made up. Use a simple, professional email address on your real résumé.

Tips

  • Start experience lines with an action verb: served, helped, organized, built, cleaned.
  • Put your most recent experience first.
  • No photos, no age, no SIN on a résumé.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Résumé Ready

Finish a full one-page résumé, get feedback from one adult outside school, and revise it. Add the final copy to your career portfolio.

3Writing a cover letterWhy you, for this job›
I can write a cover letter for a specific job. 2.4.3
A cover letter introduces you and explains why you're a good fit for this job. Three short paragraphs: who you are → why you fit → thank you.

Example Three paragraphs

Dear Hiring Manager, I am applying for the part-time grocery clerk position I saw on your store's website. I am a Grade 9 student who enjoys helping people. For the past year I have volunteered at my school's breakfast program, where I serve 40 students every morning and follow food safety rules. I am reliable, I work well on a team, and I am comfortable handling money. Thank you for considering my application. I would be happy to meet for an interview. Sincerely, Alex Martin

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Real Posting

Find a real job posting in your town (Job Bank or a store website). Write a cover letter for it, as if you were 16.

4Writing a memoShort messages inside a workplace›
I can write a workplace memo. 2.4.3
A memo is a short message to people at the same workplace. It starts with four headings: TO, FROM, DATE, SUBJECT. Then get straight to the point.

Example

TO: All class store staff FROM: Jordan Lee, Store Manager DATE: November 20, 2026 SUBJECT: New store hours Starting Friday, the class store will open for the last 15 minutes of class instead of 10. Please be at your station by 2:30. Questions? See me before Friday.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Store Memo

Write a real memo to the class about an Avalanche HQ change (new item, sale day, store hours) and post it in the classroom.

5Job applicationsOnline and on paper›
I can complete a job application accurately. 2.4.3, 2.1.3
Many employers use an application form, often online. Have your information ready before you start: dates, past experience, availability and references.

Before you apply, have ready…

  • Your résumé (to copy dates and duties from)
  • Your availability: which days and times you can work
  • 2 references: adults who aren't family and who agreed to vouch for you (a coach, teacher, volunteer supervisor)
  • A professional email address you check often

Example Availability

"Weekdays after 4:00 p.m., Saturdays all day, not available Sundays." Clear and honest, so the employer can schedule you.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Reference Ask

Ask 2 adults (not family) to be your references, using a polite email or in person. Record their contact information in your portfolio.

6Interview skillsFormal language and first impressions›
I can use formal language and body language in an interview. 4.2.1, 4.3.1
An interview is a formal conversation. Speak clearly, use full sentences, and back up what you say with a real example.

Body language

  • Sit up straight, face the interviewer, keep your hands still.
  • Make eye contact and smile.
  • Phone off and out of sight.

Example "Tell me about a time you worked on a team."

✗ "Uh, I dunno, school stuff I guess."
✓ "In our class store, I was the cashier. When it got busy, I asked a teammate to bag items so the line moved faster. We served everyone before the bell."

Situation → what you did → the result.

Questions you can ask them

  • "What does a typical shift look like?"
  • "What do your best employees do well?"
  • "When will you make a decision?"

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Mock Interview

Complete a mock interview with an adult who isn't your teacher (a family friend or a local business owner). Ask them to fill out the feedback checklist.

Unit 3

Novel vs Film

The book is almost always different from the movie, and that's on purpose. In this unit you'll read a novel (or a set of stories), track plot, character and theme, learn how filmmakers tell stories with camera, sound and light, and decide which version does it better.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • use reading strategies to stay on track with a longer text 2.1.2
  • analyze plot, conflict and character change across a novel 2.2.2
  • explain how film uses camera angle, lighting and sound to tell a story 2.1.3, 2.3.2
  • compare a novel and its film adaptation 1.1.2, 2.3.1
  • write a review that supports my opinion with evidence 2.2.1, 2.4.3
👥 Side by side: Grade 8 is mapping plots and writing scripts. Your film analysis builds right on that.

2Lessons

1Reading a longer textStrategies that work›
I can use reading strategies to stay on track with a longer text. 2.1.2
Novels are long, so use tools: predict before a chapter, question while reading, summarize each chapter in 1–2 sentences, keep a character list, and define new words in a personal glossary. Audiobooks are a great way to read along.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Chapter Log

Keep a one-line summary and one question for every chapter you read. Share your favourite question with the class.

2Tracking the storyPlot, conflict and character arc›
I can analyze plot, conflict and character change across a novel. 2.2.2
A novel often has a main plot and subplots, and several conflicts at once. The main character's arc is how they change from beginning to end. Track it with evidence: a quote or event from the start, middle and end.
BeginningMiddleEnd
What they're likeAngry, aloneStarts to trust one personPart of a team
EvidenceQuote, page #EventQuote, page #

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Arc Chart

Make an arc chart for the main character in your novel with a quote or event for the beginning, middle and end.

3The language of filmCamera, lighting and sound›
I can explain how film uses camera angle, lighting and sound to tell a story. 2.1.3, 2.3.2
TechniqueEffect
Low angle (camera looks up)Character seems powerful
High angle (camera looks down)Character seems small or weak
Close-upShows emotion
Wide shotShows the setting
Dark lightingMystery, danger
Music and sound effectsBuild mood and suspense

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Shot Spotter

Watch a 3-minute movie trailer. List 5 shots and explain what each camera angle, lighting or sound choice makes you feel.

4Book vs. movieWhat changed, and why?›
I can compare a novel and its film adaptation. 1.1.2, 2.3.1
A book can show thoughts and take its time. A film has about two hours and must show everything. So filmmakers cut characters, combine scenes, change endings or add action. Ask: What changed? Why might they have changed it? Did it work?

Compare with a Venn diagram

Only in the book · in both · only in the film. Then pick the biggest change and explain its effect on the story.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Double Feature

Pick a book and movie pair you know (or a graphic novel and its show). Make a Venn diagram and decide which version is better, with 3 reasons.

5Writing a reviewOpinion + evidence›
I can write a review that supports my opinion with evidence. 2.2.1, 2.4.3
A good review gives a short summary (no spoilers!), a clear opinion, evidence (specific scenes, quotes or techniques) and a recommendation: who would enjoy it?

Example

"The film keeps the book's heart but loses its humour. The close-ups during the storm scene are powerful, but cutting the best friend's subplot makes the ending feel rushed. Read the book first, then watch it. 3.5/5."

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Critic's Corner

Write a review of the novel or film and post it (with your teacher's help) on the school library page or in a class "Critic's Corner" binder.

Unit 4

Media & Bias

Every message comes from someone with a point of view. In this unit you'll spot bias in news and posts, judge whether a source is reliable, analyze how images and words are chosen to make you feel something, and protect your own digital identity.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • tell fact from opinion and recognize bias 3.2.2, 3.3.3
  • judge whether an online source is reliable 3.2.3
  • analyze how word choice and images shape a message 2.3.3, 4.1.4
  • identify the purpose, audience and point of view of a media text 1.2.3, 2.2.2
  • follow online safety, privacy and etiquette 3.4.1
👥 Side by side: Grade 8 is learning ad techniques and fact vs. opinion. You're taking it a step further into bias.

2Lessons

1What is bias?One-sided on purpose (or not)›
I can tell fact from opinion and recognize bias. 3.2.2, 3.3.3
Bias is leaning toward one side. Signs of bias: only one side is heard, loaded words, missing facts, opinions presented as facts, and a creator who gains something.

Example Same event, two headlines

A: "Town council approves new arena."
B: "Council wastes millions on arena nobody asked for."
B uses loaded words ("wastes," "nobody") to push a side.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Headline Flip

Find a news headline. Rewrite it twice: once biased for, once biased against. Then write the most neutral version.

2Is this source reliable?Check before you share›
I can judge whether an online source is reliable. 3.2.3
Use the CRAAP test: Currency (how recent?), Relevance (does it fit?), Authority (who wrote it, are they an expert?), Accuracy (can I confirm it elsewhere?), Purpose (inform, sell, persuade?).

Lateral reading

Instead of staying on a site, open a new tab and search who's behind it. Fact-checkers do this first. What others say about a source tells you a lot.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: CRAAP Test

Pick 2 websites on the same topic. Score each out of 5 for every part of the CRAAP test and decide which one to trust.

3Words and imagesConnotation and framing›
I can analyze how word choice and images shape a message. 2.3.3, 4.1.4
Denotation is a word's dictionary meaning. Connotation is the feeling attached to it. "Thrifty" and "cheap" mean nearly the same thing but feel very different. Images are framed, too: what's in the shot, what's cropped out, and which photo was chosen.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Crop It

Take one photo and crop it 2 ways to tell 2 different stories. Write a caption for each.

4Purpose, audience, point of viewAnalyzing a media text›
I can identify the purpose, audience and point of view of a media text. 1.2.3, 2.2.2
For any media text, ask 5 questions: Who made it? Who is it for? What's the message? What techniques grab attention? Whose voice is missing?

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Media Breakdown

Analyze one ad, music video or influencer post using the 5 questions. Present your breakdown to a Grade 8 group.

5My digital footprintSafety, privacy and etiquette›
I can follow online safety, privacy and etiquette. 3.4.1
Your digital footprint is everything you post, like and share. Employers and schools may look you up. Before posting, ask: Would I be okay with a future boss, coach or grandparent seeing this?
  • Use strong, private passwords.
  • Keep your address, school and schedule off public posts.
  • Ask before posting photos of others.
  • Disagree respectfully, or don't post.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Footprint Check

With a family member, search your name online and check your privacy settings on one app. Write 3 changes you made or will make.

3Practice

Unit 5

Persuasive Essays & PSAs

Make your case and make people listen. In this unit you'll develop a position on a real issue, back it up with evidence, write a five-paragraph persuasive essay, and turn your argument into a public service announcement.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • develop and justify a position on an issue 2.4.1
  • gather and organize evidence from credible sources 3.2.1, 3.3.1
  • write a five-paragraph persuasive essay 2.4.3
  • use transitions and strong word choice, then edit for conventions 4.1.2, 4.2.1
  • create and present a PSA 4.3.2, 4.3.3
👥 Side by side: Grade 8 is writing persuasive paragraphs and PSAs. Team up for a joint PSA campaign.

2Lessons

1Taking a positionPick a side you can defend›
I can develop and justify a position on an issue. 2.4.1
An issue has more than one reasonable side (Should phones be banned in class? Should the driving age go up?). Brainstorm both sides first. Then choose the side you can support with the strongest evidence, and write it as a thesis.

Example thesis

"Junior high students should be required to complete 20 volunteer hours because it builds job skills, connects them to the community and helps them choose a career." It states the position and previews 3 reasons.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Both Sides

Choose an issue you care about. Make a T-chart with 4 reasons for each side, then write your thesis.

2EvidenceFacts, stats, examples, experts›
I can gather and organize evidence from credible sources. 3.2.1, 3.3.1
Types of evidence: facts, statistics, examples, expert quotes and personal experience. Use credible sources (government sites, reputable news, experts) and record where each piece came from.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Evidence Bank

Find 2 pieces of evidence for each of your 3 reasons. Record the source for each.

3The five-paragraph essayIntro, 3 body, conclusion›
I can write a five-paragraph persuasive essay. 2.4.3
ParagraphWhat goes in it
1. IntroductionHook · background · thesis
2. Body 1Topic sentence (reason 1) · evidence · explain how it proves your point
3. Body 2Reason 2 · evidence · explain
4. Body 3Reason 3 (or counter-argument + rebuttal) · evidence · explain
5. ConclusionRestate thesis in new words · sum up · call to action

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Essay Draft

Draft your five-paragraph essay. Add it to your portfolio once it's final.

4Polish itTransitions and conventions›
I can use transitions and strong word choice, then edit for conventions. 4.1.2, 4.2.1
Transitions link ideas: First, In addition, However, For example, As a result, Finally, In conclusion. Check that verbs match subjects, tense stays the same, and sentences vary in length.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Read It Aloud

Read your essay out loud to someone. Fix every spot where you stumble or they look confused.

5From essay to PSASame argument, new form›
I can create and present a PSA. 4.3.2, 4.3.3
A PSA squeezes your argument into 30 seconds or one poster. Keep your strongest reason, your best statistic and a clear call to action. Pick the form that suits your audience: poster, video, radio spot or social post.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: PSA Campaign

Team up with a Grade 8 group to launch a PSA campaign at school: posters, an announcement and a short video on the same issue.

Unit 6

My Transition Plan

High school is close. In this unit you'll look at your strengths as a reader, writer and speaker, research high school pathways and careers, set goals, and put together a transition plan and portfolio to take with you to Grade 10.

Learning goalsLessonsPracticeResources

1By the end of this unit, I can…

  • assess my language strengths and set goals 1.1.4
  • research high school pathways and careers using ALIS and the NOC 1.1.4, 3.2.1
  • assess how well my research process worked 3.4.2
  • write a transition plan with short- and long-term goals 1.1.4, 2.4.3
  • present my plan and portfolio to an audience 4.3.1, 4.3.3
👥 Side by side: Grade 8 is starting their portfolios. Show them yours and give them tips.

2Lessons

1Where am I now?Language strengths and stretches›
I can assess my language strengths and set goals. 1.1.4
Look back at your work this year. Use a checklist to rate yourself on reading, writing, speaking, listening and viewing. Pick one strength to celebrate and one stretch to work on in high school.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Growth Proof

Find your first and best piece of writing from this year. Write what improved and how you know.

2Pathways and careersALIS, NOC and high school options›
I can research high school pathways and careers using ALIS and the NOC. 1.1.4, 3.2.1
In Alberta high school, you can earn a High School Diploma or a Certificate of High School Achievement. Programs like RAP (Registered Apprenticeship Program), work experience and dual credit let you start a career while in school. Use ALIS for Alberta career profiles and the NOC (National Occupational Classification) to look up job codes and duties.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Pathway Visit

Visit (or look up) your future high school's course guide. Find 3 courses or programs that connect to a career you're interested in.

3How did my research go?Thinking about thinking›
I can assess how well my research process worked. 3.4.2
Metacognition means thinking about how you think and learn. After research, ask: Were my questions good? Did I use credible sources? Did I manage my time? What would I do differently next time?

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Research Reflection

Write a 5-sentence reflection on your career research: what worked, what didn't and one change for next time.

4Writing the planShort- and long-term goals›
I can write a transition plan with short- and long-term goals. 1.1.4, 2.4.3
A transition plan answers: Where am I now? Where do I want to be? How will I get there? Include short-term goals (Grade 10), long-term goals (after high school), the steps and the people who can help.

Example

Long-term: become a heavy-duty mechanic. Short-term: take a mechanics option course in Grade 10 and ask about RAP. Steps: keep math marks up, get a part-time job, apply for RAP in Grade 11. Supports: shop teacher, career counsellor, uncle who's a mechanic. Backup plan: parts technician.

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: My Plan

Write your transition plan and share it with a parent or guardian. Ask them to add one support or idea.

5Presenting my planPortfolio showcase›
I can present my plan and portfolio to an audience. 4.3.1, 4.3.3
Present like it's a job interview: prepare, use formal language, make eye contact, and point to real evidence in your portfolio. Practise answering questions like "Why this career?"

Quick check

🧭
Expedition: Showcase Night

Present your portfolio at a student-led conference or a class showcase. Invite a family member or a mentor.

Identity & Belonging Cheat Sheet · Grade 9 Language Arts

Character analysis: choice → motive → what it reveals. Is the behaviour consistent? Is any change plausible?

Topic = one word. Theme = the message, as a sentence.

Point of view: first person (I) · third limited (one character's thoughts) · third omniscient (all-knowing).

Culture in stories: Whose voice? What values? What traditions? How would it change with a different teller?

Essay: intro (hook + thesis) → body (examples) → conclusion (restate + what it means).

Group roles: leader · recorder · timekeeper · motivator · presenter.

Getting Hired Cheat Sheet · Grade 9 Language Arts

Résumé (one page): Name and contact · Skills · Experience (newest first, action verbs, numbers) · Education · References available on request. No photo, age or SIN.

Cover letter (3 paragraphs): 1) The job you want and who you are 2) Why you fit, with a real example 3) Thank you and ask for an interview. Change it for every job.

Memo: TO · FROM · DATE · SUBJECT, then a short, clear message.

Applications: have your résumé, availability, 2 references (adults, not family, who agreed) and a professional email ready.

Media & Bias Cheat Sheet · Grade 9 Language Arts

CRAAP test: Currency · Relevance · Authority · Accuracy · Purpose. Lateral reading: search who's behind the source.

Persuasive Essays & PSAs Cheat Sheet · Grade 9 Language Arts

Thesis: position + preview of 3 reasons.

Evidence: facts · statistics · examples · expert quotes · experience. Record every source.

Five paragraphs: intro (hook, background, thesis) → 3 body (reason, evidence, explain) → conclusion (restate, sum up, call to action).

Transitions: First · In addition · However · For example · As a result · In conclusion.

PSA: one reason · one statistic · call to action · right form for the audience.
